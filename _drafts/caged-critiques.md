   neck built from what they already know.

1. What a system owes us. Four fixed criteria, with triads as a benchmark
   that passes all four:

   - Specified: can be stated so two people produce the same result.

   - Generative: a rule, not a list.

   - Delivers: usable, complete output with real content of its own.

   - Distinctive: its content belongs to it, not to the tuning or to
     everyone.

   from one to the next.

4. CAGED as visual map. Lookup table, roots only, pegboard. Its own, but

## History

Short version: the shapes are old, the name dates from the mid-1970s, and
no single inventor is established. Several sources are blogs or sellers
with a stake; confidence is noted per item.

### The underlying idea

The five movable shapes predate the name. Secondary sources say the Eddie
Lang Guitar Method (1935) and George Barnes Guitar Method (1943) build
solos from the same five shapes (plausible, unverified). Bill Edwards
reportedly said the real originator is whoever settled on standard tuning,
which matches the octave-shape point.

### Candidates for naming it (mid-1970s to early 1980s)

- Keith Allen, Blue Bear School of Music, San Francisco. A May 1975 Guitar
  Player feature ("Blue Bear Waltzes," by Jon Sievert) reportedly lays out
  five interlocking scales labeled E-D-C-A-G. Caveats: the claim comes from
  Castalia, publisher of Allen's The Guitar Poster (1980), which has a
  commercial stake; and even their account says only that the system is
  "now called" CAGED, not that the article used the name.

- Howard Roberts and GIT (Guitar Institute of Technology), late 1970s.
  Keith Wyatt says he first met CAGED as a late-'70s GIT student, through
  course material Roberts wrote. Medium confidence: secondhand report; the
  article itself couldn't be read.

- Bill Edwards, Fretboard Logic (volumes copyrighted 1983 and 1987;
  combined SE edition 1997). Reportedly first titled "The CAGED System."
  The earliest known source packaging CAGED as a complete system covering
  chords, scales, and lead patterns. Edwards reportedly said he didn't
  invent it.

- Joe Pass. Often linked to CAGED, but disputed: the claim is that he used
  the five shapes to picture chords, not as a scale system. Joe Pass Guitar
  Style (Mel Bay) was reportedly co-written with Bill Thrasher; date not
  confirmed.

### Names before "CAGED"

No single earlier name found. Teachers mostly spoke of "chord forms" and
"positions"; Berklee's Leavitt method used numbered positions. Gypsy-jazz
players reportedly use a three-shape variant called "ACE."

### Popularizers

- 1980s-90s: The Guitar Poster, GIT/Musicians Institute, Fretboard Logic.

- Internet era: Justin Sandercoe (JustinGuitar) as a major entry point,
  then the YouTube teachers (Paul Davids and others).

User's experience fits: exposure through the 1980s-2000s came from chord
books, song books, and scale-pattern books, with little CAGED talk. On this
evidence CAGED was a niche framework through the 1980s and 90s (GIT,
Fretboard Logic, a wall poster) and became ubiquitous with web and YouTube
teaching. That last step is inference, but consistent with the evidence.

### Relevance to the essay

The "system" claim appears to come from a later packaging, mainly
Fretboard Logic, layered on long-standing shapes. That fits the thesis:
the shapes are old and generic; the systemhood is the newer addition.

## What CAGED is trying to do

The answer depends on how broadly CAGED is defined. There is no canonical
definition (critique 11 below), and defenders and critics often argue about
different versions.

### The narrow version

The contents:

- Five movable shapes: the open C, A, G, E, and D major chords, made movable
  by barring or by fingering them without open strings.

- A fixed order: for any one chord, the shapes link up the neck in the cycle
  C-A-G-E-D, each sharing notes with the next.

- Root anchors: each shape has known root locations, so a shape is placed by
  finding the root on a given string.

Problem it solves: most beginners know the open chords and maybe the E-shape
and A-shape bar chords, and the rest of the neck is a blur. The tuning's
irregularity (the major 3rd between G and B) makes moving patterns around
unpredictable. CAGED builds that irregularity into shapes the student already
knows, so it teaches by connecting new material to old.

What it teaches: mainly how to see the neck spatially. It's a way of
breaking the neck into regions, not a body of musical knowledge.

What mastery gives you:

- Any major triad in five places covering the whole neck.

- Which shape sits next to which, and where the roots are in each.

- If taught with intervals, where the 3rd and 5th sit in each shape. (That
  is an extension; see "CAGED vs. a triad system.")

That's the full list: a map of one chord type, the major triad, plus a way
to divide the neck into five regions that comes with it.

### The broad version

Defenders like MacLennan mean much more by CAGED: scale patterns,
arpeggios, and minor chords all hung on those five regions.

- Pentatonic and major-scale fingerings for each shape.

- Major and minor arpeggios.

- Minor versions of the shapes (Cm, Am, Em, and so on).

- Seventh and extended chords as variations of the shapes.

None of that comes from CAGED itself. It's other material that CAGED gives
you a place to put. The scale fingerings in particular are a separate
choice, and critiques 1, 2, and 5 are really aimed at those, not at the
core.

### What it doesn't give you

- It doesn't teach note names. You only need to know the root note's
  location.

- It covers minor, diminished, and extended chords only through modified
  shapes.

- It covers scales and modes only if fingerings are added on top.

- It teaches nothing about harmony or chord function.

### The honest deliverable

A map of the neck in five regions, anchored to chord shapes you already
know, showing where every major triad and its root sit everywhere on the
neck. Everything else is material the map gives you a place to put. Whether
that structure helps or confines is what the critiques argue about.

Put more bluntly: in its narrow form CAGED teaches remarkably little. In its
broad form it is mostly empty: a set of fixed pegs on a pegboard, on which
many other topics can be hung, but with little substance of its own.

One qualification: the pegboard isn't entirely empty. Choosing to anchor the
regions on major-triad shapes is a real, if implicit, claim that chord tones
are the right frame of reference for the neck. That claim is open to
dispute.

Confidence: high on the core and the sequence. Lower on the history, such
as who coined the name and how much of the broad version early sources like
Fretboard Logic included.

## How proponents define the kernel

Concern: do the practical critiques (e.g., most shapes can't be fully
barred) lean too hard on reading CAGED as "move the cowboy chords up the
neck as bar chords"? A survey of definitional statements finds at least
four framings of the kernel, plus a possible fifth.

1. Movable bar chords. Replace the nut with a bar and play the five
   open shapes anywhere. Wikipedia (CAGED is a subsection of "Bar chord,"
   not its own article), D'Addario ("When moved up the neck, it forms
   bar chords").

2. Visual map: shapes as landmarks or regions, seen rather than played.
   Stringjoy ("visualize the chord shapes without the capo"), Premier
   Guitar ("five buckets where I can stash fretboard information"), Pickup
   Music ("fretboard landmarks"). This is the most common proponent
   framing.

3. Octave shapes. Guitar Chalk: the shapes are "made of" octaves, and
   "there are five octave shapes and each one relates to a common open
   chord that we already know."

4. Triad containers. Each shape is a grouping of major-triad voicings by
   string set. Pickup Music's triad lesson, TrueFire's "CAGED Triads,"
   Molly Miller's Guitar World material. Usually presented as an
   application of CAGED rather than its definition.

5. Tuning logic (low confidence). Fretboard Logic's subtitle, "The
   Reasoning Behind the Guitar's Unique Tuning," suggests Edwards framed
   the chord forms as consequences of the tuning. Seen via title and
   reviews only; the book itself wasn't examined.

Assessment of each framing:

- 1, bar chords: the truest kernel. It's the source of the name, and in
  YouTube teaching it dominates the initial definitions (user observation).
  The capo doesn't rescue it. A real capo makes one shape fully playable
  at one location, which is transposition, not neck mapping; it also
  blocks everything behind it, so you can't traverse C-A-G-E-D for a chord
  in real time. In CAGED teaching the capo is usually imaginary ("imagine
  a capo here," then "visualize the chord shapes without the capo"). It is
  demonstrated and then removed, leaving a visual pattern. So a proponent
  who invokes the capo has already retreated to framing 2. It also
  sharpens the critique: the bar was supposed to be a movable nut, and
  for most shapes a finger can't do a capo's job.

- 2, visual map (movable patterns shaped like cowboy chords): a less
  strict variant of 1. It concedes some Shapes aren't very playable but
  keeps them as a visual structure to hang other things on. More
  defensible, but it slides quickly into 3; that slide is visible in
  several of the sources above.

- 3, octave shapes: even more defensible, but CAGED is lost. Every system
  must use and adhere to octave shapes.

- 4, triad containers: a better foundation, but the Shapes aren't fully
  triadic (they merge power chords and triads), and it raises the question
  of what CAGED adds that a triad-based framework wouldn't handle better.

- 5, tuning logic: if the forms are presented as consequences of the
  tuning, this collapses into 3. Set aside.

Structure: 2 and 3 are relaxations of 1, each dropping a requirement
(playability, then chord shape). 4 is a substitution: a different
foundation under the CAGED name. Relaxation leaves less and less of CAGED;
substitution leaves only the name.

Implications:

- The bar-chord reading is the truest kernel, though proponents often
  retreat to 2 or 3 when defending it. The "can't bar most shapes"
  critique hits framing 1 squarely and the others only indirectly.

- Against framing 2 the practical critique survives in its stronger form,
  as the transfer premise: the cowboy chords are only a visual mnemonic,
  which advertises a motor familiarity it doesn't deliver (see "Visual
  vs. motor transfer").

- Framing 3 is answered in "The octave-shape defense," framing 4 in
  "Building blocks: triads and power chords" and the chunking collapse.

- Pattern: each framing retreats further from the cowboy chords, and the
  more defensible it gets, the less CAGED-specific it is. That's a
  motte-and-bailey. The bailey ("you already know these chords; just move
  them") does the selling; the motte (octaves, triads, visualization) does
  the defending, and belongs to everyone.

Side note on Wikipedia's thin coverage: Wikipedia tracks citable secondary
sources, and CAGED lives mostly in the commercial lesson market, with
little scholarly or reference literature. The CAGED subsection itself cites
a MusicRadar lesson, a 1999 David Mead book, and Tom Hess. So its
modest footprint there likely reflects sourcing, not importance.

## CAGED vs. a triad system

Taking the "system" claim seriously means comparing CAGED against an
alternative, stated with equal strictness and no extensions.

### Triad system (user's statement)

- Define it precisely and compactly.

- Rule:

  - Pick a key/root (e.g., C).

  - On each set of three contiguous strings, find the 1-3-5 chord tones.

  - Those are your triad inversions.

- Learn that small library of movable shapes for each chord quality: major,
  minor, etc.

Anyone fluent in it would know a lot of useful, playable material. Not
everything about music (no system offers that), but a massive advantage
over their prior self.

### CAGED, framing 2 (visual map), stated as strictly

Framings 5 (unclear), 4 (not true: the Shapes aren't fully triadic), and 3
(not CAGED-specific) are set aside. Framing 1 is the truest kernel; 2 is
the more robust. Framing 2 stated narrowly:

- Pick a root (e.g., C).

- Take five fixed inputs: the open C, A, G, E, and D major chords, each
  with its known root locations.

- Slide each shape until its root sits on the chosen root, treating the
  nut as an imagined capo at that fret.

- The five placed shapes, in order C-A-G-E-D, cover the neck. Those are
  your five regions.

- Learn the five shapes as visual patterns; playability not required.

### Where the strict statement goes soft

- Inputs are a list, not a rule. The triad system takes chord quality as a
  parameter. CAGED's inputs are five specific open chords that exist as
  easy open chords only for major (Cm and Gm aren't open chords; Am, Em,
  and Dm are). The triad system's "etc." line can't be written for CAGED.
  This isn't an extension; it's the same parameter the triad statement
  includes.

- Shapes aren't fully defined: which strings belong to A, C, and D is left
  to the teacher (see "Coverage gaps").

- No note roles. The triad rule labels every note 1, 3, or 5 as part of how
  it works. A CAGED shape learned as a picture carries only its root;
  knowing which dot is the 3rd requires added interval teaching, an
  extension.

- "Seen, not played" is written into the definition. The triad system's
  output is playable by construction.

### Two systems compared

    Feature         | Triad System                | CAGED System
    ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    Definition      | Exact and easily stated     | Rarely defined with precision
    Generation rule | Chord tones + string sets   | Octave shapes + open-chord lookup
    Chord qualities | All: maj, min, dim, aug     | Major; others vary by shape
    Shapes          | 12                          | 5
    Playability     | All playable; most are easy | Varies by shape
    Chord tones     | All: 1, 3, 5                | Focus is on roots
    String coverage | Exhaustive                  | Varies by shape
    Organized by    | String set and triad stack  | Position (neck region)

### Neck regions: not distinct either

CAGED provides neck regions: five vertical slices. But the triad system
has its own regions. Triads on adjacent string sets share two strings and
glue into "triad stacks" (strings 1-2-3 joined to 2-3-4, and so on down to
4-5-6). Because of the tuning, the stacks run at an angle across the neck
rather than straight up and down. They follow almost directly from the
triad rule, so they count as own-content, not an extension.

What remains of CAGED's contribution is only orientation: vertical rather
than diagonal. And vertical windows can be derived from triads too: the
triads on every string set within one 4-5 fret window yield the notes of a
CAGED shape.

Fair point for the vertical slice: it keeps the hand in one position,
which suits position playing, reading, and chord-melody; diagonal stacks
involve shifts. A real trade-off, but a choice of slicing, not CAGED
content.

Concession: vertical regions are genuinely useful, since the guitar itself
is vertically defined at the nut. The triad system doesn't do everything.
The fair claim is that triads connect to vertical regions, not that they
make them unnecessary.

### Triads connect outward

A triad orientation connects well to what a player already knows (cowboy
chords) and to what they learn later (advanced voicings, other scales). For
example, a player learning triad stacks can find the stack that overlaps
most with the CAGED E shape and see exactly where the two diverge.

Worked example, C chord, E shape at fret 8 (8-10-10-9-8-8, C G C E G C):

- Strings 4-3-2: triad C-E-G (frets 10-9-8), matches the E shape.

- Strings 5-4-3: triad G-C-E (frets 10-10-9), matches.

- Strings 6-5-4: the E shape has C-G-C (frets 8-10-10), a power chord, not
  a triad. The stack gets its third note by moving string 6 to E at fret
  12.

Divergence is on string 6. And the note the stack moves to (E, string 6,
fret 12) is the same note the coverage-gap example found no standard CAGED
shape includes. The triad stacks fill the gap the CAGED shapes leave:
triads reach the CAGED shapes, but not the reverse.

### Triads extend to other chord qualities

This is an extension, outside the own-content comparison, but it shows how
far triad knowledge reaches. Easy 3-string voicings (quasi-triads, or
"triadic" voicings) that play or imply many other chord qualities are each
one note away from a known triad:

    Target  Move from the triad        C example
    sus2    3 down to 2                C D G
    sus4    3 up to 4                  C F G
    maj6    5 up to 6                  C E A
    min6    5 up to 6 (from minor)     C Eb A
    dom7    1 down to b7               Bb E G
    maj7    1 down to 7                B E G
    min7    5 up to b7 (from minor)    C Eb Bb

Physical echoing: many of these voicings are the same physical shapes as
known triads. C-E-A is an A minor triad; C-Eb-A is A diminished; Bb-E-G is
E diminished; B-E-G is E minor. Context (usually the bass) decides the
name. So triads extend in many directions, and the extensions reinforce
each other: learning the moves also teaches what each familiar shape can
mean.

Example: minor triads over a dominant 7th. Over A7 (A C# E G):

- Em (E G B) gives 5, b7, 9: an A9 sound.

- F#m (F# A C#) gives 6, 1, 3: an A6 sound.

Same idea as jazz "upper structures."

Contrast with CAGED: extending to 7th chords means modifying each 5- or
6-string grip shape by shape. Triads get there by one-note moves on three
strings, uniformly across string sets.

On terminology: "Triad System" is used here only as a foil for critiquing
CAGED. The alternative doesn't need to be called a system at all; it's
just triads.

### Bottom line

Method: restrict each system to its own content (no extensions) and ask
what it delivers. Triads: a lot. CAGED: a little, with lingering questions.

The key difference: CAGED's own content teaches you only roots. The triad
system teaches the locations of 1, 3, and 5; learning the inversions bakes
those facts into the method. Any note roles beyond the root in CAGED come
from added interval teaching, an extension.

Stated strictly, CAGED framing 2 is a five-item lookup table with a
placement rule. For major chords it has roughly the same note content as
triads, but with less defined units, no note roles, and no way to vary
chord quality. Even its neck regions have a triad counterpart (stacks),
leaving only the vertical orientation, which triads can also produce.

## The major-only problem

CAGED is major chords only. That's fine as a starting point, analogous to
starting the triad system with majors. The problem is what happens when
you try to extend the five Shapes to other chord qualities.

- A, E, D: convert to minor easily. Am, Em, and Dm are standard cowboy
  chords.

- C: Cm in cowboy form (e.g., x31013) is playable but not easy. It's
  rarely seen, though not impossible or unheard of. C diminished in cowboy
  form doesn't work.

- G: the most problematic. An open Gm voicing exists, 310033 (G Bb D G D
  G), and it's playable, but not easily, and it isn't a standard cowboy
  chord. Barred up the neck it's a no-go: an index bar on strings 4-3, a
  note one fret up on string 5, and three notes three frets up on
  non-adjacent strings 6, 2, and 1. In practice, a movable Gm means
  converting to the E shape. The G Shape has no usable movable minor form.

Contrast with the triad system: chord quality is a parameter. Lowering the
3rd gives minor, and lowering the 5th as well gives diminished, uniformly
on every string set and inversion. CAGED's inputs are five specific open
chords, so for minor its procedure partly runs out of inputs (see "CAGED
vs. a triad system").

## Is CAGED systematic?

Working thesis: CAGED is not very systematic. It lacks coherence, resists
precise specification, and has no clear boundaries.

Scoping the claim: the narrow version is quite systematic. Five shapes, a
fixed order, and placement set by the root: two teachers working from that
will produce the same result. The charge holds against the broad version.
The sharper claim is that CAGED is either precise or substantial, never
both. Scoping it this way also blocks the defense that CAGED is just five
shapes.

### Candidate criteria

- Specifiable: could it be written down so two teachers produce the same
  thing? The narrow version passes. The broad one fails: authors disagree on
  where each shape's scale pattern begins and ends (critique 11).

- Generative: is the content produced by a rule, or just listed? The shapes
  are listed. They're a historical accident of which open chords happen to
  be easy, not derived from a principle. 3NPS fingerings and the interval
  approach both come from a single rule. This criterion goes to whether
  CAGED is a system at all or just a collection of shapes.

- Uniform: does it treat all cases alike? No. It covers major chords only,
  the minor versions of the shapes vary in how playable they are, and the
  scale patterns mix two and three notes per string.

- Bounded: is it clear what's inside and outside? Not for the broad version,
  as in MacLennan's "not even 1 percent of what it actually is."

- Consistent mapping: do the five regions hold up across different
  material? Pentatonic boxes map onto the shapes cleanly, major-scale
  patterns less so, and the regions overlap anyway.

- Exhaustive: does it cover the neck? Not as chord shapes. See below.

### Coverage gaps

Three of the five shapes leave out strings in their usual forms:

    Shape   Usual open form   Strings covered
    E       022100            all 6
    G       320003            all 6
    A       x02220            5-1 (sometimes 6, using the low E)
    C       x32010            5-1 (sometimes 6, using the low G)
    D       xx0232            4-1 (sometimes 5, using the A)

A gap you can check: take a C chord, with the D shape at fret 10 and the C
shape at fret 12. The E at fret 12 on string 6 is a chord tone sitting in
the D region, and no standard shape includes it.

This connects to two of the other criteria but is distinct. Which strings a
shape includes is optional and varies by teacher, which is a "specifiable"
failure. The shapes also span 6, 6, 5, 5, and 4 strings, which is a
"uniform" failure. But "exhaustive" is a separate test, and a pointed one,
because covering the whole neck is CAGED's main selling point.

The predictable defense is that the scale patterns in the broad version
cover strings 5 and 6 in every region. That's true, but then the coverage
comes from added material, not from CAGED: a concrete instance of the
pegboard point.

How much it matters: in practice players fill these gaps without thinking,
so it's more a hit to the claim that CAGED covers the neck than a problem in
actual playing. For an argument about CAGED as a system, that claim is the
right thing to target.

## The transfer premise

CAGED starts from shapes the student already knows (open "cowboy" chords)
and promises they can be moved up and down the neck. But the open-chord
shapes don't move well:

- E: yes. Movable as a whole and in part.

- A: only partly. Fluent players rarely move the full shape (x02220 shifted
  up). They drop the high E string (x2444x) or play top-three-string
  triads (xxx224). The A shape retreats into triads and practical
  power-chord shapes.

- C: same, but more so. Some tunes use a true bar of the C shape, but it's
  an intermediate-to-advanced voicing to execute fluently. Triad subsets
  are far more practical and common.

- D: doesn't use all six strings and isn't readily movable. The full moved
  shape (xx2454) is sometimes used but isn't common. Players use the
  top three strings (xxx454) or nearby voicings (xx6454) that shade into the
  C shape. A practical failure and a conceptual mess.

- G: probably the least movable. Triad subsets, yes (Hendrix). A bar G
  shape, almost nobody.

One out of five is a poor batting average for a system whose first promise
is transfer.

### The defense, and what it gives up

Defenders will say CAGED never claimed you'd play full shapes; the shapes
are frames for seeing the neck, and subsets are the intended use. That's
the best reply available, but it concedes that the unit actually played is
the triad (or power chord), not the shape. Those still have to be learned
separately, and the premise "you already know these shapes" helps only to
find the frame, not to play what's in it.

### Visual vs. motor transfer

Moving an open chord up the neck turns every open string into a fretted
note, so the fingering changes. Motor transfer (the familiar fingering
still works) happens cleanly only for E. Visual transfer (recognizing the
pattern) works for all five. So the premise holds only as visual
recognition: a frame you see, not a set of grips you can use.

### Building blocks: triads and power chords

The CAGED shapes are not simply unions of triad inversions. They contain
the same pitch classes as triads, but as voicings they merge power-chord
shapes with triadic ones. By adjacent three-string sets:

- E (022100): strings 6-5-4 are E-B-E, a power chord with octave; the
  upper strings are triadic.

- A (x02220): strings 5-4-3 are A-E-A, a power chord.

- D (xx0232): strings 4-3-2 are D-A-D, a power chord.

- C (x32010) and G (320003): triadic throughout.

So power chords and triads together produce the CAGED shapes. Both are
generated by simple rules (string set and inversion), and triads extend
uniformly to minor, diminished, and augmented chords, inversions, and voice
leading. The shapes are derivative, not foundational: starting from triads
and power chords would yield all the same knowledge, and more, without
the CAGED frame.

### What survives: almost nothing

- Chunking: initially credited as CAGED's one real benefit (grouping
  unfamiliar triad grips into five containers with familiar names). On
  scrutiny it collapses:

  - Triads can be taught in chunks as big or small as a student can
    handle, and tied directly to the cowboy chords. The top three strings
    of open C, A, G, E, and D are triads: "this is a triad, now move it."

  - Triads have their own sequence rule. CAGED's C-A-G-E-D order looks
    like a unique feature, but on each string set triad inversions cycle
    root, first, second. That's cleaner, because it holds for every string
    set and chord quality.

  - The familiarity CAGED leans on belongs to the open chords, not to
    CAGED, and triads get it for free.

  - Triads never lose relevance as a player advances. CAGED stumbles on
    consistency from the start ("wait, I can't easily move these shapes")
    and loses relevance later.

- Full bar chords: the E- and A-shape bars (in practical forms like
  x2444x) are real full-chord rhythm voicings. But every method teaches
  them, CAGED or not, so they aren't CAGED's contribution.

Precise conclusion: CAGED's own contribution is a five-part grouping of
triads and power chords that are better taught directly, and even the
grouping offers nothing triads lack. A label, not a system.

### On calling it bogus

Redundancy alone shows CAGED is dispensable, not bogus. "Bogus" needs it to
mislead, and the case for that is the transfer premise itself: the promise
that open chords carry over up the neck breaks down as soon as you leave
first position.

## CAGED at advanced levels

Observation: excellent players (Guthrie Trapp and Molly Miller are the cases
in mind) give lessons billed as CAGED-related, but the connection holds
only in the sense that (a) they say it does, and (b) the notes on the neck
dictate where any system must find its ingredients. The connections work
only if you don't examine the logic carefully.

Thesis: such players are unreliable narrators of their own method. This
critiques the label, not the musicianship. One can admire the playing while
discounting the account of it.

Supporting points:

- Research: people are poor at reporting the processes behind their own
  skilled behavior. Classic source: Nisbett and Wilson, "Telling More Than
  We Can Know" (1977). Expertise research finds similarly that experts'
  explanations are often reconstructed after the fact. Unreliable narration
  is the normal case for experts, not a slight.

- Unfalsifiable coverage: the five regions cover the whole neck, so any
  lesson on any notes lands inside some CAGED shape. A framework that can
  claim every lesson is like a test no one can fail: the connection costs
  nothing and proves nothing. This ties back to the "bounded" criterion and
  doesn't depend on judging anyone's intentions.

- Market incentives (speculative): "CAGED" is a heavily searched term, so
  tagging a lesson with it brings in viewers whether or not the lesson uses
  it. Present as speculation, or leave out.

Pushback considered: players' own accounts that they visualize the neck in
CAGED regions are evidence of something, namely CAGED as a personal frame
of reference. The defensible claim is not that they don't use it, but that
the frame does no work triads, intervals, and chord tones couldn't do. At
advanced levels CAGED survives as a label and a visualization habit, while
the musical work is done by other things.

To do for the post: pick one or two specific Trapp or Miller lessons and
take the claimed CAGED connection apart step by step. Without a concrete
example, the claim reads as assertion.

### Jens Larsen: CAGED-labeled scale diagrams

Several pages on Larsen's site are framed as CAGED material, but they're
the usual scale diagrams with root notes colored red. That's not a use or
demonstration of CAGED in particular. The connection is the same empty one:
the roots must be somewhere, and every system must place them.

## Case study: Paul Davids

Source: [Paul Davids on CAGED][pd] (YouTube). Notes are the user's, from
rewatching the video; not independently checked.

Davids is one of the more influential guitar teachers on the internet, and
a reasonable, sensible one, judging from a lot of his material. That makes
him a strong test case: a fair-minded proponent, not a salesman.

- Definition: the usual one, shifting familiar open chords up the neck via
  a bar (framing 1).

- He admits people tend to change fingerings when doing this. The result
  looks more like partial triad stacks than true shifts of the CAGED
  chords.

- G shape: he admits people rarely play it as-is. His workaround is a
  partial triad stack. In effect he says: granted, you aren't playing the
  full G shape, but the shape helps you visualize nearby notes that belong
  to the chord. True, but it undermines the system's contribution: the
  utility comes from playable triad structures, not from a proper G shape.

- D shape: "This is a ... useless shape -- it's really uncomfortable"
  [11:23]. His decision: just use the closed triad.

Significance:

- The lesson enacts the motte-and-bailey in real time: it opens with
  framing 1, retreats to framing 2 (visualization) for the G shape, and
  lands on triads for the actual playing.

- It's first-hand support for the transfer-premise critique (G and D
  shapes don't move) from a proponent, not a critic.

- It partly answers the to-do under "CAGED at advanced levels": a
  concrete, examinable lesson where the claimed CAGED connection does
  little work and the triads do the rest.

## Generative systems vs. CAGED labels

A truer system: take stated rules or concepts and use them to generate
something practical that covers the full neck in a movable way. The scale
patterns often shown under the CAGED label can be generated that way, and
CAGED contributes little to them.

- Pentatonic: five boxes follow from a rule. Five notes, two per string,
  start a box on each scale note on string 6, adjust at the B string. The
  result is five boxes covering the neck without reference to any chord
  shape. Their alignment with CAGED shapes is a consequence of the tuning,
  not of CAGED.

- Diatonic: the five classic patterns also follow from a rule: take the
  five pentatonic boxes and fill in the two missing scale degrees (4 and 7)
  within each position. The count of five comes from the pentatonic's five
  notes, not from chord shapes. (An earlier draft credited CAGED with
  fixing the count at five; that was wrong.)

Consequences:

- Critiques 1, 2, and 5 (mixed notes per string, poor extension to other
  scales) really target the "pentatonic plus two" scheme. The mixed 2/3
  notes per string comes from starting with 2NPS boxes and filling in.
  Those critiques hit CAGED only because the label is attached.

- Cowboy shapes could generate different scale diagrams: fill in scale
  tones around each chord shape's own footprint. The results would not
  match the five patterns normally labeled "CAGED scale patterns."

- The D shape is the starkest case. Its footprint covers only strings 4-1,
  but a scale pattern must cover all six, so the "D-shape scale pattern"
  borrows its low strings from elsewhere. It's a pattern labeled with a
  shape it can't be derived from.

Result: the classic scale patterns owe nothing to CAGED beyond the
narrow, mathematical sense that roots must be somewhere. The CAGED label on
them is borrowed credit.

Open question (historical): when and how the five pentatonic and diatonic
patterns acquired the CAGED label.

## The octave-shape defense

The defense: CAGED isn't really about cowboy chords; its kernel is octave
shapes (root locations across strings). The chord shapes merely align with
those roots.

Verdict: it doesn't rescue CAGED. It trades "unsystematic" for "trivial,"
moving CAGED onto the precise-but-tiny horn of the dilemma.

What the octave-shape version gets right: it's coherent, fully
specifiable, and exhaustive. The count of five even has a clean derivation:

- Within any 12-fret span, each string has a given pitch class exactly
  once: six root locations.

- Of the open strings E A D G B E, only the two Es share a pitch class, and
  they always share a fret.

- So every pitch class falls on exactly five distinct frets per octave.
  C's roots: string 2 fret 1, string 5 fret 3, string 3 fret 5, strings 6
  and 1 fret 8, string 4 fret 10.

Why it fails as a rescue:

- The five comes from the tuning, not from the open chords. Octave shapes
  are what they are; every system works within them and every guitarist
  who learns octaves gets them. They don't distinguish CAGED from other
  systems.

- It gives up what made CAGED distinctive: root geography carries no chord
  type and no chord tones, no beginner on-ramp from familiar shapes, and
  the name "CAGED" no longer describes anything.

- It escapes some critiques (transfer premise, coverage gaps, major-chord
  bias), but only by becoming content that belongs to everyone.

A real contribution: names. On reflection the dismissal of framing 3 was
slightly too quick. CAGED attaches labels to the octave shapes (the five
root regions), named for the root octaves found in familiar open chords.
The naming is clumsy ("an A-shape E chord at the 7th fret"). Alternatives
(from general knowledge, not researched):

- Fretboard Logic's "C form," "A form": only superficially different from
  "C shape."

- Root-string labels (R6, R5, R4, as in jazz chord books): don't uniquely
  identify the five regions. E and G both have their lowest root on string
  6; A and C both on string 5.

- Numbered positions (pentatonic "Pattern/Box 1-5"): about something
  slightly different (scale patterns), and the names are poor. Numbers
  aren't names, especially with no strong basis for where numbering
  starts (it differs depending on whether you count from major or minor).

- String-pair names: the only alternative that works. Name each region by
  its root octave's strings: "the 6-4 octave," "play a 5-3 A chord." Not
  a crowd pleaser, but not too clumsy, and the names carry real meaning.
  (The user has toyed with this in their own music-theory notes.)

String-pair names identify all five regions uniquely. Root octaves per
region, for a C chord:

    Region   Root octave      Frets
    C        5-2              str5 f3, str2 f1
    A        5-3              str5 f3, str3 f5
    G        6-3 (also 3-1)   str6 f8, str3 f5
    E        6-4              str6 f8, str4 f10
    D        4-2              str4 f10, str2 f13

Each region has an octave pair no other region has. And the scheme names
the thing itself, the octave, rather than pointing to it through cowboy
chords. It names framing 3's defensible kernel without framing 1's
baggage.

### Seven octave shapes

Refinement (user's): the E region also contains two octave shapes (6-4 and
4-1), like G. And pure octave shapes have a low string and an orientation,
right (toward the body) or left (toward the nut), from the player's
perspective:

    Shape   Pair   Frets   Crosses G-B?
    6R      6-4    +2      no
    6L      6-3    -3      no
    5R      5-3    +2      no
    5L      5-2    -2      yes
    4R      4-2    +3      yes
    4L      4-1    -2      yes
    3R      3-1    +3      yes

Two rules generate all seven:

- R: octave two strings up, +2 frets (+3 when crossing G-B).

- L: octave three strings up, -3 frets (-2 when crossing G-B).

There is no 3L, since no string sits three strings up from string 3. The
6-1 double octave (same fret) is a chain of two shapes: 6L + 3R, or
6R + 4L.

CAGED regions as bundles of octave shapes:

    C = 5L
    A = 5R
    G = 6L + 3R
    E = 6R + 4L
    D = 4R

So the seven octave shapes are the real primitives, and CAGED's five
regions are a grouping of them. This scheme is a genuine system: precise,
generative, exhaustive, and meaning-bearing. It inverts the usual picture:
framing 3 isn't CAGED's defensible kernel; it's a separate, better system
that CAGED loosely packages.

### Talking vs. thinking

Problem: the octave-shape scheme never stuck in the user's own notes
because it's so abstract and technical. How would one guitarist tell
another how to play a specific voicing in this lingo?

- It can't, really. "5R" names where the roots are, not a voicing: not
  which other notes, which strings, or the grip. Specifying a voicing
  would need extra layers ("E major, 5R region, root on 5, then 5th, root,
  3rd above"), and nobody talks like that.

- CAGED names carry a picture. "A-shape E at the 7th" calls up a grip both
  players know (roughly x79997 or a subset). The names work because they
  point to a shared image, the cowboy chord, which is framing 1. Irony: the
  framing that fails in practice (the shapes don't really move) is what
  makes the names work in conversation (both players can picture them).

- How players actually communicate voicings: fret numbers or tab
  ("x-7-9-9-9-x," exact and universal); CAGED names ("A shape at the 7th,"
  quick and approximate); triad terms ("E triad, top three strings, root
  on top," exact and about as compact). The triad system's vocabulary works
  in conversation too.

- Position naming: "an E in 7th position." The user's actual habit, and
  it beats CAGED names:

  - Anchored: a position number is a fret number, not an arbitrary label
    like Box 1-5.

  - General: works for any chord quality, scale, or line, so CAGED's
    major-only problems don't arise.

  - Established: standard in classical and Berklee-style teaching.

  - Equally loose about the voicing: "E in 7th position" and "A-shape E"
    both leave the listener to choose among x79997, x7999x, or a triad.

  CAGED's residual edges are small. "A shape" also says the root is on
  string 5, which a position number doesn't, but that helps only someone
  who already thinks in shapes. Position and region boundaries don't align
  exactly ("E in 9th position" could be the A-shape region's top or the
  G-shape region's start), but players resolve that by context.

Each scheme fits a different job:

- Octave shapes: good for analysis and thinking (a real system), poor for
  talking.

- Position names: good for talking, general, and anchored.

- Triad terms: good at both, and exact about the voicing.

- CAGED names: not clearly best at anything. What remains is pedagogical
  (a picture for beginners who know only cowboy chords) and social (widely
  shared jargon). The social value is partly circular: the vocabulary is
  useful because it's popular. And the pedagogical value rests on the
  familiar-picture premise of framing 1, the part that fails in practice.

Thesis in compact form: CAGED is a vocabulary that gets mistaken for a
system, and not even a necessary vocabulary.

Conclusion: CAGED's naming is a real but modest contribution. It isn't the
only workable scheme; its advantage over string-pair names is familiarity,
which helps pedagogy but isn't essential. A useful vocabulary, not a
system, and not even an irreplaceable vocabulary.

Small qualification: one remaining distinctive claim is methodological,
namely organizing the neck around root locations rather than fingering
(3NPS) or intervals. That's a stance, not a system, and it doesn't need
the CAGED shapes.

## The critiques

Bracketed tags refer to the sources below.

1. Mixed 2- and 3-notes-per-string fingerings make picking inconsistent and
   speed harder to build. [Z] [H]

2. Picking is inefficient: the picking hand travels more than needed. [H]

3. Major-chord bias: minor, diminished, augmented, and altered chords are
   awkward. [Z] [H]

4. Poor fit with arpeggios: the shapes are hard to play cleanly as
   arpeggios, and the method pushes scale-based playing. [Z] [H] [G9]

5. Doesn't extend to harmonic minor, melodic minor, or exotic scales; each
   needs a new set of patterns. [Z] [H]

6. Players get stuck in vertical boxes, and solos sound boxed-in.
   [Z] [H] [G9]

7. Splitting the neck into five regions is arbitrary and doesn't divide it
   logically. [G9]

8. "Only five patterns" is misleading: you still have to learn where each
   scale degree sits. [Z]

9. Guitar-only vocabulary. "C shape at the 7th fret" means nothing to other
   musicians, while terms like triads and drop-2 chords carry over.
   [H] [PG] [GF]

10. Redundant and commercial: it repackages what the tuning already gives
    you, and it's marketed as a system to sell books and courses. [GF] [Z]

11. Defined inconsistently, and the claims about famous players are myths:
    Hendrix predates it, Joe Pass used six patterns, Petrucci plays 3NPS.
    [Z]

12. As a teaching method it builds dependence on familiar shapes and doesn't
    by itself produce creativity. [PG]

13. It teaches the neck piecemeal rather than as one unified picture, and the
    barred G and C shapes are painful and rarely needed by hobby players.
    [PL]

14. For scale work, thinking in notes is more useful than thinking in
    shapes. [GF]

## Overall verdict

Even at its most minimal, defensible framing (a vocabulary for talking
about octave shapes and the voicings that fit them), CAGED is weak tea.
Simple phrasing like "an E in position 7" does the same work without
claiming a "system."

Is there a modest beginner value to concede? On scrutiny, no. Candidates:

- The "aha" that shapes repeat up the neck: real, but triads tied to
  cowboy chords deliver the same one (move the top three strings of an
  open D).

- E- and A-shape bar chords: every method teaches them.

- A learning sequence: triads have one too; the chunking advantage already
  collapsed.

- Popularity (abundant free material, shared jargon): a social fact about
  CAGED, not value in the method.

A better concession: grant the need, dispute the solution. CAGED's
popularity reveals a real beginner need, a map of the neck that grows out
of what students already know. The need is genuine and CAGED addresses it;
the argument is that triads meet it better. That keeps the post fair
without crediting CAGED with value the analysis can't find.
