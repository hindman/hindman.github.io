# Essay

## Why triads?

I have played guitar for nearly 50 years, and for the first 40 if someone
asked me to play a basic chord like Am I would have reached mainly for two
options:

    [diag: standard Am voicings: open strings and at 5th]

I understood in an abstract way that there were other ways to voice the chord,
but I had no fluency with them and they did not affect how I thought about
what I was playing.

At the same time, I lacked the ability to fluidity navigate the fretboard when
trying to improvise. I had learned the common scale diagrams for the major
scale and its modes, as well as the major pentatonic, minor pentatonic, and
blues scales. Depending on a song's key, I could move around with some freedom
on limited parts of the fretboard, but there were not many comforts zones and
I could easily find myself lost.

Now things are much better. I can fluidly play an Am using nine shapes, all of
them practical, spannning across the full neck. I can do the same for any
major or minor chord. Those shapes are not just a collection of grips that one
might see in a standard chord book. To the contrary, the shapes are
foundational: they help to organize my knowledge of music theory and, even
more important, provide a lens through which I visualize the fretboard. I tend
to see most chord voicings in relation to the major and minor triads. When
learning songs I locate the melody or solo notes relative to the relevant
triads. And when improvising, the triads provide likely landing spots and
reference points.

The internet has no shortage of triad lessons, some quite good. But most begin
by handing you the shapes and telling you to memorize them. It is good advice,
as far as it goes, but this essay tries to provide a sharper rationale for the
triad shapes that everyone teaches and points toward some of the connections
you can make between triads and other chords.

## Triad construction

A triad is a three-note chord built by stacking two 3rd intervals: start on a
note (the chord root), add the note a 3rd above it (the chord's 3rd), then the
note a 3rd above that (the chord's 5th). Stacking 3rds is the basis of most
chord building in Western music, so triads are where nearly every chord
begins. If intervals, chord tones, and scale degree are unfamiliar, see Marco
Carillo's [overview][yt_carillo], an efficient music theory primer written for
guitarists.

[yt_carillo]: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5EfGXzz3DGc

A 3rd interval comes in two sizes: major (four frets) and minor (three frets).
Stacking two of them in every possible way yields four combinations, each
producing a different kind of triad, called its quality. Triads vary in
quality based on their 3 and 5 chord tones, which can be natural, flat (b3 and
b5), or sharp (#5).

| 3rds | Triad quality | Chord tones |
| ---- | ------------- | ----------- |
| M M  | Augmented     | 1 3 #5      |
| M m  | Major (M)     | 1 3 5       |
| m M  | Minor (m)     | 1 b3 5      |
| m m  | Diminished    | 1 b3 b5     |

This essay focuses on triads in close position (close triads, for short),
where the three notes sit — you guessed it — as close together as possible, on
adjacent strings. Close triads are easy to play, widely used, and readily
connected to one another across the neck. Finally, there are three ways to
arrange a triad's chord tones in a playable form on adjacent strings. Those
arrangements are called inversions.

| Triad tones  | Inversion         |
| ------------ | ----------------- |
| 1 3 5        | Root position     |
| 3 5 1        | First inversion   |
| 5 1 3        | Second inversion  |

## Triads, by string set

An effective way to organize your learning of the triad shapes is to begin
horizontally along the neck — in other words, learn the triad inversions along
one group of adjacent strings. There are four such string sets. I recommend
starting on the highest string set, `3-2-1`, and working down. In many musical
styles, the two upper string sets are where much of the action happens: chord
voicings that cut through, plus the melodies and solos that sit on top of
them.

| String set |
| ---------- |
| 3-2-1      |
| 4-3-2      |
| 5-4-3      |
| 6-5-4      |

We can start with a root position triad on string set `3-2-1`:

    [img: C maj triad, root position, strings 3-2-1]

Starting from any chord voicing, to find its next highest inversion is simple:
on each string, advance the chord tone to the next one up the neck. In our
current example, the `1` advances to `3` (on string 3), the `3` advances to
`5` (on string 2), and the `5` advances, in wrap-around fashion, to `1` (on
string 1). That gives us the first inversion C major triad with chord tones `3
5 1`. Applying the same procedure again produces the second inversions, `5 1
3`. Doing it again gives us `1 3 5`, a root position triad an octave above
where we started.[^1]

If we apply that inversion process to all four string sets, we can generate C
major triads across the full neck.

    [img: C maj triads, all string sets]

Since a minor triad differs from a major triad by only one note (a `b3` rather
than a natural `3`), the minor triads can be memorized as small variations of
the major triads we started with. The other two triad qualities involve
similar kinds of adjustments: the diminished triads are like the minor triads,
but with a `b5`; and augmented triads are like major triads, but with a `#5`.[^2]

[^1]: The inversion process also works in the other direction (down the neck)
    and with larger chords. For example, a common dom7 voicing is `1 x b7 3 5
    x` (`x` marks an unplayed string). Proceding string by string, advance
    each chord tone to get `3 x 1 5 b7 x`, the first inversion. Two more steps
    produce the second and third inversions. Also note that when looking at
    the triad inversions one might be tempted to think that inversion is a
    process of rotating chord tones across the relevant strings; and it is for
    close triads. But rotation does not describe the process for different
    types of triads (for example, spread triads) or bigger chords (as shown in
    the dom7 example). So the correct way to think about inversion is via the
    string-by-string method just described.

[^2]: The augmented triads are further simplified due to their internal
    symmetry, where all of the chord tones are exactly 4 frets from their
    neighbors. In practical terms, that translates into having to memorize
    only one shape per string set.

## Triads, by stack

If string sets organize triads horizontally on the neck, stacks organize them
vertically. A stack extends a triad's chord tones across all six strings, for
example `1 3 5 1 3 5`, low string to high. Any three adjacent strings in a
stack hold a close triad, so a stack is just four of the string-set shapes,
overlapping in one region of the neck. And because a stack is a longer run of
chord tones, the same string-by-string inversion process can be used to
convert any stack into its neighbor. That is why there are three stacks: the
six-string run of chord tones can start on `1`, `3`, or `5`.

    [diag: maj triad stacks]

Although none of the stacks are easily played in full, four-string and
five-string subsets of them are, and they overlap significantly with common
chord grips you probably already know.

Beyond playability, however, is the bigger point: the entire network of triad
shapes — running horizontally on string sets and vertically in stacks —
gives you a neck-spanning grid for visualizing the fretboard and keeping
yourself oriented.

## Triads, by inversion

When you learn triads, memorizing the shapes tends to go reasonably fast. What
lags is knowing what is inside them: fluently grabbing a shape without
immediately knowing which of its notes is the root, or which inversion you are
playing. This diagram groups the triads by inversion (the rows) across all
string sets (the columns):

    [diag: major triads, as a grid by inversion]

In each row, notice what stays the same and what changes. The constant is the
intervals between each pair of chord tones. Pay attention to the shapes of
those intervals and you will learn to connect the memorized shapes to the
inversion name and, thus, its chord tones.

| Inversion        | Chord tones | Intervals |
| ---------------- | ----------- | --------- |
| Root position    | 1 3 5       | M3, m3    |
| First inversion  | 3 5 1       | m3, P4    |
| Second inversion | 5 1 3       | P4, M3    |

What varies within the rows are the shapes — due to the way a guitar is tuned.
The rule is simple to state: when a shape moves to a higher string set, the
chord tone that lands on the B string moves up one fret; and when moving to a
lower string set, the chord tone that lands on the G string moves down one
fret. Becoming fluent in this kind of shape shifting is not easy, but close
triads are an excellent vehicle to practice the skill.

## Practice advice

You now have three ways to organize the major triads (by string set, by stack,
and by inversion) and links to the same material for minor, diminished, and
augmented. That might look like a big pile, but it is really one small set of
shapes viewed from different angles. The practical questions are where to
start and how to proceed.

Organize the practicing into stages:

  - Major triads: string sets. Start with the highest string set and then work
    your way down through the sets.

  - Major triads: stacks. At this point the shapes will be known; now you are
    learning to connect them vertically.

  - Minor triads. Repeat those two steps for minor triads as one-note
    variations of the major shapes.

  - Diminished triads. Repeat again for diminished triads, this time as
    one-note variations of minor shapes.

  - Augmented triads. Learn them last; they are less common, and there is only
    one shape per string set.

Learn more than just the chord shapes:

  - Chord tones and inversion name. Playing the triad's notes string by string
    — fingerstyle or with a pick — is a good way to focus your mind on this
    dimension.

  - Shifts to neighbors. Practice shifting from any triad to its same-triad
    neighboring shapes, both along a string set and within a stack.

  - Note names. Do not get bogged down in these details in the beginning.
    Start with shapes, inversions, and chord tones — in other words, the
    triads as generic, moveable units. That said, practicing triads in
    specific keys, especially the common guitar keys, is a good way to build
    up note name fluency across the fretboard.

Practice routines:

  - Progression using every voicing. Start with any chord progression
    involving triad qualities you have learned. For each chord in the
    progression, play every triad voicing (either on a string set or within a
    stack) at a steady tempo you can maintain.

  - Harmonized major scale. If you know how to harmonize a major scale with
    triads (see the [Carillo][yt_carillo] video), work on playing the full
    triad scale on a string set. This can be done horizontally, along the full
    neck, but even more useful is to play a chord scale as compactly as
    possible within one position on the neck. For this type of practice, you
    can stay on one string set or use a couple of them to keep the neckwise
    motion even more confined to one position.

  - Cycle of 4ths. Start with any triad and then proceed through all triads by
    moving up a perfect 4th (for example: C, F, Bb, Eb, Ab, Db, Gb, B, E, A,
    D, G, and back to C). There are various ways to do this, but I find that
    working within a position is especially useful, because it teaches you to
    know where nearby chords reside. An interval cycle like this can be done
    with any triad quality, but start with the most common: major and minor.

  - With either of the first two practice approaches, use a bass note as a
    pedal tone, typically the root of the key you are working in. If needed,
    alter the tuning of the low E or A strings to facilitate that.

## The power of triads

Because the technical explanation for triad construction is quite simple, and
because some readers might already know this material, it is easy to gloss
over how powerful this knowledge can be. We started with C major triads and we
have 12 voicings to memorize (or 9 unique shapes, since the two lowest string
sets are structurally identical). But those shapes are **moveable**, which
means the same shapes work for all major triads. In other words, at the cost
of memorizing a tiny amount of information, we have a way to play every major
triad across the full neck. And the shapes are easy to play. Indeed, they are
easier to play than most of the full-grip, open-position cowboy chords shoved
at beginner guitarists in most guitar education. Easy to memorize, easy to
play, versatile (all major triads, not just specific grips), and exhaustive
(covering the full neck). If you have ever used an old-school guitar chord
book, you realize how misconceived and impotent they were relative to teaching
a beginner about triads.
