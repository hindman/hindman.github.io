# Essay

## Why triads?

I have played guitar for nearly 50 years, and for the first 40 if someone
asked me to play a basic chord like Am I would have reached mainly for two
options:

    [diag: standard Am voicings: open strings and at 5th]

On an academic level I understood that there were other ways to voice the
chord. But I had no fluency in playing them and they did not affect how I
played or how I thought about what I was playing.

At the same time, I lacked the ability to fluidity navigate the fretboard when
trying to improvise. I had learned the common scale diagrams for the major
scale and its modes, as well as the major pentatonic, minor pentatonic, and
blues scales. Depending on a song's key, I could move around with some freedom
on limited parts of the fretboard, but there were not many comforts zones and
I could easily find myself lost.

Now things are much better. Without hesitation I can fluidly play an Am using
nine shapes that are immensely practical and that span all strings across the
full neck. I can do the same for any major or minor chord. Those shapes are
not just a collection of grips that one might see in a standard chord book. To
the contrary, the 9 shapes are foundational: they play a substantial role in
how I organize my knowledge of music theory and, even more important, they
are the lens through which I visualize the fretboard. I tend to see most chord
voicings in relation to the core major and minor triads. When learning songs I
locate the melody or solo notes relative to the relevant triads. And when
improvising, the triads provide likely landing spots and reference points that
help me know what the nearby scale tones are.

The internet has no shortage of triad lessons, some quite good. But most begin
by handing you the shapes and telling you to memorize them. It is good advice,
as far as it goes, but this essay tries to provide a sharper rationale for the
triad shapes that everyone teaches and points toward some of the connections
you can make between triads and other chords.

## Planned structure

1. Introduction. The author's story: 40 years with two Am grips, then 9
   shapes that now organize how the neck is seen. The claim: triads as the
   lens for seeing the fretboard. What the essay offers: a systematic route
   in, a practice recipe, and pointers onward.

2. Prerequisites. Short. Name the assumed vocabulary (note names,
   intervals as fret distances, scale degrees) and delegate it (Marco
   Carillo and similar).

3. Building and arranging triads. State the method: start from every
   possibility, then explain why we narrow. Stacking two 3rds gives four
   qualities; major and minor are the core, diminished and augmented named
   with their roles noted briefly. Three tones have six orders; three are
   practical as closed voicings on adjacent strings. Define inversion here,
   without rotation language. Spread triads mentioned as the road not taken
   and delegated (Alex Farran).

4. Triads on the neck: string sets. Four string sets, three inversions
   each. The B-string adjustment explains why shapes change across sets.
   The 9-vs-12 framing lives here. Diagrams for major, then minor as a
   one-note change.

5. Triad stacks. Adjacent string sets join into regions of the neck. The
   open chords the reader knows contain these triads: an on-ramp from what
   they already play.

6. More chords: triad neighbors. The table of 12 one-move neighbors, filtered
   to the useful ones. Emphasis on the two dom7 moves. Physical echoing: many
   neighbors are known triad shapes in disguise. Brief link to established
   terms (shell, rootless, upper structure).

7. More chords: overlaying triads. Here the idea is to take basic triad (maj,
   min, dim, aug) and overlay it on top of some other harmony. This
   is "upper structure triads" -- a top covered a fair bit by others --
   but extending in a more systematic approach. Two examples I like
   and use a lot: (A) over an A7 harmony, plan Em and F#m triads (ie,
   Vm and VIm over a I7); and (B) take a minor harmony and slide its
   own triad up 2 frets (ie, over Im play a IIm). There are many other
   examples. A systematic listing of the options, highlighting the
   useful ones I know about, could be interesting. TBD.

8. A practice recipe. Pedal key, note-by-note playing, steady tempo.
   Stages: string sets, then stacks, then the 4ths cycle (later with the
   dom7 move). Harmonizing the major scale enters only as far as the cycle
   needs. Our own etudes go here if any make the cut.

9. Where next. Musical applications delegated to Farran, Alon Albagli, and
   Jimmy Brewer, with a sentence or two on what each offers. Link to the
   CAGED essay later.

  needs a term.

- In/out test: include material that explains how triads are built or how
  they sit on the neck; delegate vocabulary the reader needs going in.
  Examples in: the four ways to stack 3rds; the six arrangements of 1-3-5,
  narrowed to three. Examples out: interval and scale detail.

The systematic principle:

- The essay's distinctive value. Triad material is abundant online; little
  of it explains why these shapes and not others.

- The author's objection to "you gotta learn your drop-2 voicings": the
  first question is why those voicings. Where appropriate, show the
  procedure: enumerate every possibility, then explain the narrowing.

Rotation: avoid loose talk of inversions as rotations. True for closed
triads, false for spread triads and for larger voicings (dom7, maj7, min7),
even in closed form.

Triad neighbors:

- Rarely taught: perhaps one triad video in a hundred covers it. Likely
  the piece's most distinctive section.

- The two one-move dom7 sounds from 1 3 5: lower the 1 to get b7 3 5
  (rootless; also a diminished triad), or raise the 5 to get 1 3 b7
  (shell). The 1 5 b7 voicing ("pow7") is two moves from the triad, or one
  from the shell.

- Enumeration fits the systematic principle: each tone moves up or down a
  half or whole step, giving exactly 12 neighbors. From major 1 3 5:

      Tone   down 1/2    down whole   up 1/2      up whole
      1      7 3 5       b7 3 5       b2 3 5      2 3 5
             (maj7)      (dom7)       (b9)        (add9)
      3      1 b3 5      1 2 5        1 4 5       1 #4 5
             (minor)     (sus2)       (sus4)      (#11)
      5      1 3 b5      1 3 4        1 3 #5      1 3 6
             (b5)        (add4)       (aug)       (6)

  Minor gets its own table. Two-tone moves can be mentioned briefly.
  Unverified: playability of each neighbor on every string set (some
  half-step clusters may be awkward).

- Terminology: no standard term exists. Nearby terms: incomplete chord
  (classical), partial voicing or chord fragment, three-note voicing,
  shell voicing and rootless voicing (specific cases), upper-structure
  triad (a triad over another root), implied harmony (the concept, not
  the voicings). None names the procedure of moving one tone of a triad.
  Candidates for our own term: triad neighbors, near-triads, one-move
  voicings. Undecided.

Practice recipe:

- Note-by-note playing ("pima" loosely; a pick works too), not strummed
  chords. Why: it keeps attention on chord tones rather than vague grips,
  and sets the stage for melody and soloing over triads.

- What worked for the author: first the shapes on each string set, then
  fluency moving vertically (stacks). The 4ths cycle is likely the most
  pleasing and useful form. The author's earlier notes found progression
  and triad-pair etudes less useful than cycle-2 and cycle-4 exercises.

- Each 4ths-cycle step is a V-I move, so lowering the current chord's root
  to b7 makes it V7 of the next. That pairs the recipe with the most useful
  neighbor.

- Pedal: keys with open-string roots (E, A, D) need no retuning; retuning
  (e.g., drop D) is optional.

- Open: diatonic cycle (C F Bdim Em Am Dm G C; stays over one pedal, brings
  in minor and diminished) or chromatic (all keys, loses the pedal).

- Musical applications beyond the recipe are delegated to Farran,
  Albagli, Brewer.

## Insights from the Brooks Robertson etudes

Motivation: theory is easy to present and grasp on a mental plane. The
hard part is fluency, moving up and down the neck playing triads and stacks
musically. No set of etudes has been found that is both doable and musical
enough to draw a learner into real practice time. Brooks Robertson's
TrueFire course on right-hand rolls did that for a physical skill: the
etudes were musically compelling, and the music, not the abstract benefit,
was the magnet for weeks of daily practice.

Two etudes examined (Guitar Pro exports, MusicXML):

- Forward Motion: key of C, 189 bpm, Intro / A / A / Outro. Forward roll
  (p-i-m) throughout.

- Backward Funk: key of B minor, 196 bpm, A B A C D A B C D A B, Outro.
  Backward roll (high to low).

Key finding: they are triad etudes in disguise. Nearly everything the left
hand plays is a three-note shape on one adjacent string set (mostly 4-3-2
or 3-2-1). Examples:

- Forward Motion intro: triads on 4-3-2 move under an open high E (an F
  triad plus E gives an Fmaj7 sound), with a creeping bass on string 4.

- Forward Motion bars 15-18: an F triad held while the top string
  alternates D and E (F6 and Fmaj7 colors from one moving note).

- Backward Funk section B: B-D-F# becomes Bb-D-F# (minor to augmented by
  one note, a line cliche).

- Backward Funk section D: a G triad alternates with G-Bb-C#, a
  diminished sound acting as a rootless A7b9. One-note moves set to a
  groove.

### What makes them compelling

1. Each etude is a tune: title, style, sectional form, intro and outro, an
   ending.

2. One fixed right-hand pattern; the left hand carries the variety.
   Repetition is built in without boredom.

3. Groove from rhythmic variety, not a plodding stream of eighth notes. The
   main devices:

   - 3+3+2 grouping of a three-note roll in 4/4, so accents shift.

   - Anticipations: a note on the and-of-4 tied over the bar line, so the
     chord change arrives early (the push).

   - Rests and stabs: bars that break off on beat 3 or 3.5; held block
     chords as punctuation.

   - Off-grid figures: grace-note slides that shift following notes onto
     sixteenth-note offbeats; an occasional triplet.

   The right-hand pattern is constant, but its placement in the bar keeps
   changing. Listening again confirmed this is a main source of the
   appeal.

4. Open-string pedals and drones give small shapes big harmony (maj7, add9,
   sus colors).

5. Small left-hand moves: chromatic bass lines and line cliches change one
   or two notes at a time. Little finger travel, sophisticated harmony
   (diminished and augmented passing chords included).

6. Melody on top: a top-string note lands with the bass on the downbeat and
   is held while the roll fills in, giving a melody-plus-accompaniment
   illusion from one pattern.

7. Short repeated sections (4-8 bars), learned in chunks.

8. Rationed ornaments: a few slides, hammer-ons, grace notes.

9. A tempo worth reaching: sounds fine slow, exciting at speed.

### The harder problem for triad etudes

Robertson's etudes develop a physical skill (rolls), so he had free rein
over harmony. Triad etudes develop a mental and visual skill (seeing and
knowing shapes), and the student can't be assumed to know all triads and
quasi-triad forms (E6, E7, etc.) yet. So the early etudes are necessarily
constrained harmonically: the harmony is the curriculum.

What still works under that constraint:

- Groove, form, pushes, rests, stabs, and drones are independent of shape
  knowledge.

- Harmony from context: a plain triad over a pedal sounds richer with no
  new shape (B over open E suggests Emaj9; A over open E suggests a sus
  sound).

- I, IV, V, vi with major and minor triads cover a lot of folk and pop.

- Each etude should make one shape family salient (e.g., the same chords
  in two inversion regions), with musical interest carried by the
  shape-independent dimensions.

A possible sequence:

1. Early: one key, one string set, major and minor triads only (I, IV, V,
   vi), moving through inversions; color from pedals and rhythm.

2. Middle: more string sets and stacks; add ii and iii.

3. Later: diminished and augmented triads.

4. Last: one-note-move quasi-triads (6, 7, sus) and line cliches.

### Etude design principles (current thinking)

Status: drafts are not finished products, but "in striking range." A
beginner learning triads would find the current best draft (v4 below)
entertaining enough to build fluency.

1. The harmony is the curriculum. Early etudes use only shapes the student
   is learning: diatonic major and minor triads on one string set.

2. Pick a progression where the next chord is always nearby. E B F#m A
   works well: all diatonic, sounds good, minimal voice movement on
   strings 3-2-1. It's an embellished E B A A: F#m (A C# F#) is an A6 in
   disguise, so F#m to A is a one-note move (F# down to E).

3. Cover every inversion. On one string set each chord has three shapes,
   so a 12-bar section in three regions (low, middle, high; one phrase of
   four bars each) shows every chord in all three inversions.

4. Rhythm is the main lever, but not to excess. Use two templates, paired
   as call and response, rather than a different pattern every bar:

   - Call: eighth-note arpeggio on strings 3-2-1 grouped 3+3+2, ending
     with a push (the next chord struck on the and-of-4 and tied over the
     bar line).

   - Response: the pushed chord rings, a syncopated hit on the and-of-2,
     then a tail.

5. Frame the etudes widely, not just for fingerstyle players. Playable
   with pick or fingers: single-note arpeggios and three-string hits, no
   finger markings. Keep bass-plus-melody coordination out of it; an
   occasional bass note is fine at convenient spots (open strings on beat
   1).

6. Tail melodies for variety and transitions. Each response bar ends with
   a short scale line on string 1 that leads by step into the next chord's
   top note (a device Robertson uses: play the roll, then climb the scale
   on the high E). The tails do double duty: they carry the hand between
   regions (climb at the end of a phrase, descend before the repeat). They
   also establish that triads are reference points in a larger network of
   scales and bigger chords.

7. One simple left-hand ornament: the one-note move (F#m to A) as a
   pull-off. Across the three regions it falls on string 1, then 2, then
   3, so the same idea is seen moving through the shapes.

8. Keep it doable. Over-varied versions (bass on every beat, six
   right-hand templates in eight bars) drifted toward
   intermediate-to-advanced fingerstyle and were dropped.

## Visualize the fretboard through triads

NOTE: These notes started as a direct copy of the material in the writing-vft
section of my guitar-notes file.

Intro and motivation

The basics:

    To discuss triads, we need a few building blocks. I will assume only a
    little prior knowledge of music theory. To address any gaps, see Marco
    Carillo's brief, practical, and guitar-focused overview.

        https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5EfGXzz3DGc

    The first building block is an interval, which describes the difference in
    pitch between two notes. That difference can be described in various ways,
    but on a guitar perhaps the most natural measure is the number of frets.
    Here are the core intervals, their numbers (often used when describing
    chords and scales), their Roman numerical representations (often used when
    describing chords in a progression), and their fret distances.

        # What is the relevance of roman notation at this point??

        Interval | N | Roman | Frets
        ----------------------------
        Unison   | 1 | I     | 0
        2nd      | 2 | II    | 2
        3rd      | 3 | III   | 4
        4th      | 4 | IV    | 5
        5th      | 5 | V     | 7
        6th      | 6 | VI    | 9
        7th      | 7 | VII   | 11
        Octave   | 1 | I     | 12

    The second building block is a scale, which is a musically useful
    collection of notes. A scale can be characterized in terms of its intervals
    -- specifically, the intervals between the first note of the scale (often
    referred to as its root or key) and all of the other notes. The intervals
    listed above coincide with the most prominent example: the major scale.

    In many musical contexts, the major scale is the reference point against
    which other scales are compared. For example, we could start with the major
    scale intervals listed above and alter the 7th interval by lowering it one
    fret (notated as b7). Or we could start with the major scale intervals and
    then raise the 4th interval (notated as #4). Or we could create scales with
    fewer notes, either with or without alterations. Such examples are listed
    below using the interval numbers (also called scale degrees). Their names
    are also shown in the table, but neither they nor the scale/mode
    distinction are important for now.

        Scale degrees  | Scale
        ---------------------------------------
        1 2 3 4 5 6 7  | Major scale
        1 2 3 4 5 6 b7 | Mixolydian mode
        1 2 3 #4 5 6 7 | Lydian mode
        1 2 3 5 6      | Major pentatonic scale
        1 b3 4 5 b7    | Minor pentatonic scale

    The fourth building block is a chord. Like a scale, a chord is a musically
    useful collection of notes that are played either simultaneously or nearly
    so. A song's chords provide the harmonic context within which its melodies,
    licks, and solos acquire their musical meaning. Like scales, chords can be
    described in terms of their intervals -- again, the intervals formed
    between a chord's key or root and each of the other chord tones. A chord's
    3rd interval is often the most important and determines whether it has a
    major or minor sound. Those two broad categories can be further divided
    into finer classifications based on the other intervals contained in the
    chord. You probably know various chord forms already. A few examples are
    listed below with their names and symbols -- again, the examples are merely
    illustrative, so don't get bogged down in the details.

        Chord tones | Symbol     | Name
        ---------------------------------------
        1 3 5       | .         | Major
        1 b3 5      | m         | Minor
        1 3 5 7     | maj7      | Major 7
        1 b3 5 b7   | m7        | Minor 7
        1 3 5 b7    | 7 or dom7 | Dominant 7
        1 3 5 b7 #9 | 7#9       | Dominant 7, #9
        1 b3 5 6    | m6        | Minor 6

Triads

    The two most common chords (major and minor chords) are also known as
    triads, which are three-note chords formed by stacking consecutive 3rd
    intervals. Since a 3rd interval can be major (4 frets) or minor (3 frets)
    we can derive four possible types of triads:

        3rd intervals | Triad quality | Chord tones
        ----------------------------------------
        Major Major   | Augmented     | 1 3 #5
        Major Minor   | Major         | 1 3 5
        Minor Major   | Minor         | 1 b3 5
        Minor Minor   | Diminished    | 1 b3 b5

    Just as we can desribe a scale in terms of the intervals it contains, we
    can do the same using a scale's triads. The procedure here is to build a
    triad starting from each major scale tone (the first column). Each triad is
    formed by starting with the triad root and then selecting the other two
    scale tones in an every-other-one fashion (the second column). To determine
    the type of each triad, we rewrite those scale tone from the perspective of
    the triad's root (the third column). Those triad tones determine the
    triad's quality (the last column). As shown in the table, the major scale
    consists of seven triads: 3 major, 3 minor, and 1 diminished.

        Root | Scale tones | Triad tones | Triad quality
        ------------------------------------------------
        1    | 1 3 5       | 1 3 5       | Major
        2    | 2 4 6       | 1 b3 5      | Minor
        3    | 3 5 7       | 1 b3 5      | Minor
        4    | 4 6 1       | 1 3 5       | Major
        5    | 5 7 2       | 1 3 5       | Major
        6    | 6 1 3       | 1 b3 5      | Minor
        7    | 7 2 4       | 1 b3 b5     | Diminished

Learning the major triads: preliminaries:

    The most practical way to start learning all the ways to play triads
    is to organize the guitar into four sets of adjacent strings.

        String set
        ----------
        6 5 4
        5 4 3
        4 3 2
        3 2 1

    Throughout this discussion we almost never learn a single chord grip;
    rather, we will learn the chord's form (major triad in this case) and how
    to play that chord in every possible arrangement of the chord tones on all
    string sets.

    There are six ways to arrange the tones in a major triad (135, 153, 315,
    351, 513, 531) but only three of them have practical shapes on a
    contiguous string set. Those three arrangements are also known as
    inversions: we start with the root form, which has the chord root as the
    lowest tone (135); and then create the other two arrangements by rotating
    the tones.

        Triad tones | Inversion
        -----------------------
        1 3 5       | Root
        3 5 1       | First
        5 1 3       | Second

        ** NOTE regarding last sentence above. Inversions are not created via

    triad and its two neighbors. By repeating this approach across all keys,
    you will cover all of the pairings multiple times.

- Geometry of the guitar:
    - Shapes are a way of organizing our memory of how to play
      groups of notes.
    - The shapes of the triads in the E triad etude, for example.
    - How do we transfer those shapes to other notes.
    - For example, how would be play the A triads?
    - Option 1:
        - shift up 5 frets (or down 7).
        - Works great: our prior knowledge transfers directly.
    - Option 2:
        - Shift to another string set: eg, 321
        - Basic rules to shift notes or shapes across strings.
            - Upward shift: notes landing on B string => +1 fret.
            - Downward shift: notes leaving B string => -1 fret.
    - So to move a triad shape we know: 432 => 321.
        - Shift the shape as is.
        - Then +1 anything on B string.
        - Practice this mentally, so you can visualize the shifts happening.

- 7 chords: stacking 3rds continues:
    - Triads have three notes: eg, 1 3 5
    - A 7-chord has four: 1 3 5 7
    - To get a four-note chord, you stack up three 3rds.
    - There are 8 ways to do that:
        - table of all chord types
        - don't dwell on all of the types now

- dom7 chords: a triadic approach.
    - even though a dom7 chord is defined to have 4 notes, a musician
      does not need to play all 4 in every context.
    - other instruments might be involved
    - and sometimes things sound better (or are more practical
      to play) if notes are dropped
    - By dropping 1 note from a dom7 chord, one can play
      a triad-like approximation for the chord.
    - There are four ways:
        1 3 5 .     # triad
        1 3 . b7    # shell voicing (drop the 5)
        1 . 5 b7    # pow7
        . 3 5 b7    # rootless (also dim triad)
    - Each of those triadic approximations is musically useful
      in a context where the dom7 sound is desired.
    - It can be helpful to be able to express a desired harmony
      with simpler fingerings (3 notes rather than 4).
    - A good way to practice these voicings is
      to play them sequentially up and down the neck
      on a string set.

    - And you can visualize the connections by starting from
      the triad and then making small changes to get the
      next chord in the sequence.

      rootless       TRIAD       shell      power      rootless       NEXT-TRIAD
              b7 <= 1     5 => b7     3 => 5     1 => 3        b7 => 1

    - It is a 12-shape sequence through all ways to play dom7 triads.

    - Etude: Am => E7.

- From triads to chord extensions and soloing:

    - We visualized the dom7 triads as simple alterations
      from the major triad shapes we already knew.

    - The same approach can be taken to explore and ultimately become
      fluent in most other types of chords.

    - In this approach, the triads operate like prominent landmarks on
      the fretboard. Other chords are generated either by adjusting one
      of the triad tones, or by adding additional tone(s) to the triad.

    - An example:
        - Start with a minor triad on 432.
        - Explore that position of the fretboard.
        - Find the practical chord voicings in that area.

    - Just as we can visualize many other chords in the neighborhood
      surrounding any triad shape we know, we can so something similar
      when creating melodies, playing solos, or improvising.

    - An example:
        - The underlying harmony for the current part
          of a song is Am.
        - Start with a minor triad on 432.
        - Explore that position of the fretboard thoroughly.
        - Start experimenting with the position to find its strengths
          for creating interesting melodies, licks, etc.

    - Repeat both types of exploration for all minor and major triad
      shapes, especially those on strings 432 and 321.
