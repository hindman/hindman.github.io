## Inventory

Skills:
  - Rolls (arpeggio patterns): sequential single-note rolls: pim or pima.
    - Static or ascending/descending string sets.
      - 2 pim; static string set
      - 4 pim; ascending string sets
    - Ascending/descending rolls.
      - 5 mip; ascending string sets; descending roll  # challenge
    - Simple or mixed etudes:
      - 10 pmi ami pmi  # challenge
    - full pima
      - 90 p a m i p i m a

  - Pinch plus roll:
    - Like the etudes above, but the rolls use ima and the thumb also
      plays on one or more beats within the roll.
    - Rolls can vary (as above).
    - Pinch beats can vary.
    - Either simple or mixed etudes patterns.
    - Examples:
      - 11 ima, pinch beat 1
      - 16 pmi ami

  - Compound patterns: multiple fingers simultaneously (partial chords)
      - Similar to pinches, but more than 2 fingers in the pinch
      - As etudes advance, they tend to use multiple techniques
      - Examples:
        - 18 pma p i : 3-note pinch then p i
        - 23 pma p ima  # More complex

  - Grand roll:
    - Like rolls, but across more strings so one repeats p during
      ascent and i during descent.
    - 89 p p i m a m i i

  - Finger pairs:
    - With thumb:
      - 17 pi : basic example
      - 98 pi mi
      - 101 pi ai ai ai mi pi pi pi
    - Without thumb (classic is i-m alternation):
      - 81 p i m i
      - 82 p m i m
      - 83 p i m i  pa i m i
      - 85 p i a i  p i m i
    - Combo:
      - 25 pi mi pi  # fun and a good exercise to get thumb to higher strings
      - 27 pi pi mi pm im am
      - 29 pi ai mi
      - 30 pi pm ia   pa im pi   # various techniques
      - These combos verge into rolls.
        - 31 pi ma mi   # pim ami
        - 32 pa mi ma   # pam ima

  - Tremolo preparation: patterns like p-a-m-i
    - 100 p a m i

  - Alternating bass:
    - 36-50
    - Examples:
      - 36 pm i p i    # simple
      - 40 pm im p i   # harder

  - Thumb independence:
    - Static interval with im on every 16th note in 4/4 time.
    - The bass moves around -- asending, descending.
    - Bass also hits non-intutive beats -- ie not first 16th in each 4-note cluster.
    - 51-65
      - G really covers most of the combos.
      - He doesn't do that with other families of etudes.
    - Examples:
      - 51 pim im im im   # Simple
      - 54 im im im pim   # Bass on 4th note
      - 56 im pim pim im  # tricky combos: bass on 2nd and 3rd

  - Chord event independence (or chord insertion independence):
    - 66-80
    - Lower voice (via p and i) is the backbone across the batch.
    - 3-note chord hits are the variable, coming on various beat(s).
    - Examples:
      - 66 pma p i p
      - 68 p p ima p
      - 78 pma p ima pma

Favorites exercising various skills:

  - 94: ditto: roll plus economy plus motion
  - 95: mi and pi workout; lost of ascend/descend
  - 96: pi then pami (tremelo)

## Background

I'm starting to work on a new post for The Fifth Fret (F5). The topic
is Giuliani's 120 studies for right hand:

  - I have a .gp file of all of them to share with others.

  - I worked through the exercises and got a lot out of them and want to try
    to explain my sense what it did for someone in my situation.

  - My background. Quite a bit of fingerstyle experience as a hobbyist; but
    self/internet/book taught so no extensive technical work.
  - In particular, my RH approach was to favor strong fingers: if i-m can do
    it, use them; otherwise, involve finger-a only when needed.
  - Plus some basic facility in p-i picking.
  - Known weaker areas before starting:
    - Finger-a not as confident, consistent, fluid. Not hopeless, but a clear
      weak spot.
    - Lacked ability to play i-m and m-i consistently. Tendency to rely on
      repeating same finger across descending string shifts (drag, rake,
      sweep, or economy fingering). My RH finger choices were mostly intuitive
      -- aside from the most troublesome passages, where I would/could work
      out a needed fingering. But this was not a setting I could control with
      full accuracy: eg, play a 2-octave scale up and down with strict i-m or
      m-i.
    - Some specific finger combos were tough. These would crop up when
      learning new tunes, and I would solve each specific case at hand.

  - The 120 exercises did not revolutionize things for me.
    - But I did notice real changes in a positive direction.
    - Finger-a much improved.
      - I find myself adjusting how I play songs I've known for years, and I
        can percieve and hear the improvement: more stability and confidence
        and/or better sound over some of the passages where I stopped working
        around finger-a.
      - On new tunes I'm much more likely to use finger-a than I would have in
        the past.
    - Some i-m/m-i improvement.
      - I perceived this effect as real but less striking than the one for
        finger-a.
      - I'll return to this subject in another post.

    - More significant but also a bit mysterious:
      - My RH is just better.
      - I perceive it as more settled, more in control.

  - I also want to discuss the exercises themselves:
    - Some of this is descriptive:
      - The exercises can be grouped topically.
      - I'm not aiming for exhaustive categorization.
      - Rather I'm thinking to come at it in this order:
        - Specific a RH skill seen in the etudes:
          - Provide illustrative example or two -- with image of the
            tab/notation.
        - Repeat that for all the skills the etudes suggest to me.
    
    - And some of this discussion would be things like:
      - Mentioning a few favorite examples.
      - Highlighting etudes that seem especially relevant for fingerstyle
        players who might not be game for the full 120.
    
    - Now that I think about it, I guess my approach here is the same as the
      one I used for my first rudiments post: some description and also some
      suggestions for subsets of the full 120 that people might want to
      pursue.

## CHAT

A few thoughts on structure and motivation:

**The motivating frame is the fingerpicker crossover.** You've developed it
well in our conversation: here's what a self-taught fingerpicker lacks, and
here's what classical etudes systematically address. That's more compelling
than "I did these exercises and they helped." It gives readers a reason to
care before you describe anything.

**The "Background" section is the essay — it's just written as notes.** Most
of what you need for the opening is already there: your starting point, your
specific weaknesses, what changed. It just needs to become prose.

**Structural suggestion — same spine as your rudiments post, but with the
personal frame as the hook:**

1. Brief intro: who you are as a player, the crossover premise (fingerpicker
   encounters classical etudes)
2. Practical note: .gp file available
3. Skill tour: each category with notation examples, brief notes on what's
   hard and why, occasional "fingerpicker translation" (e.g., "this is the
   classical name for what you know as a pinch")
4. Favorites and subset recommendations — earns its place after the tour
5. Closing reflection: the mysterious general improvement — save it for last
   because it's your most interesting and honest observation

**Two cautions:**

- The taxonomy is detailed enough to become a dry catalog. The
  fingerpicker-translation frame prevents that — it gives each section a
  human angle.
- The "mysterious general improvement" deserves more than a sentence. That's
  the part readers will find most resonant, and it's the hardest to explain,
  which makes it worth sitting with.

**One structural question worth deciding:** do you want the personal results
(finger-a improved, etc.) up front as motivation, or do you reveal them at the
end after the tour? Front-loading them gives readers a reason to read the
taxonomy; saving them makes the ending more satisfying. Your rudiments post
— how did you handle it there?

On the Giuliani context: the key facts are brief. He was an Italian virtuoso
(1781–1829), based for much of his career in Vienna where he was
well-regarded alongside Beethoven and Hummel. He was among the most prominent
guitarists of the early 19th century classical guitar revival. The 120 studies
are a purely mechanical exercise: left hand holds a simple chord throughout
each study, isolating the right hand entirely. That design is the key thing to
convey — it's not music, it's a laboratory. I'm less certain about the opus
number; worth a quick verification before publishing.

One framing thought for the context section: the "laboratory" quality of the
studies is what makes them unusual and worth explaining. Most etudes have both
hands working. These strip the left hand out completely, which is either
maddening or liberating depending on how you approach it — and that's worth
flagging for a fingerpicking reader who may expect something more musical.
