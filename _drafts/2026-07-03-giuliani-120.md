## Drills of a different color

The [first post][fingerstyle_rh_rudiments] at The Fifth Fret covered
right-hand rudiments for fingerstyle guitar: a set of 256 exercises I created
to build fluency in the alternating-bass patterns common to blues and folk
picking. This post is a continuation of that endeavor, but the source material
is different: classical guitar etudes written over two centuries ago.

[Mauro Giuliani][wiki_giuliani] was one of the leading classical guitarists of
the early 19th century. His "120 Right-Hand Studies" (Op. 1, Part 1) were
published in 1812 and have been a staple of classical guitar education ever
since. Segovia reportedly used them as a daily warmup. The design is simple:
every etude consists of two bars, one of C and one of G7. The early exercises
tend to focus on specific right-hand patterns or skills. The latter exercises
combine those patterns in various ways.

Collectively, the 120 etudes leave few corners of right-hand technique
unexplored. A steady diet of tunes, even a varied one, tends to favor your
existing strengths and route around your weaknesses. These exercises do not.
Outside a few technique-minded genres, such as bluegrass banjo and shred
guitar, the online guitar world is a sea of things to play, with only a few
islands of rigorous technique building. Classical guitar pedagogy, by
contrast, has spent two centuries working out how to build a player's
foundation from the ground up, and the Giuliani 120 are one of its pillars.

I came to these exercises as an intermediate fingerstyle player -- blues,
folk, and some jazz, roughly in the vein of Tommy Emmanuel. I had years of
exerience, but was mainly self-taught via books and then internet. My right
hand policy, to the extent that I had one, was to favor the strongest fingers
— index and middle -- and to resort to the ring finger only when a tune
demanded it. In addition to some known weakness in the ring finger, I also
lacked the ability to play single-note lines with strict index-middle
alternation — a workhorse of classical guitar technique. Instead, I would
select right-hand fingering intuitively, often relying on what one might call
economy (or rake/sweep) fingering in the right-hand, such as repeating the
index finger while descending from string to string. Finally, if a right-hand
maneuver caused any trouble when learning a song, I would solve each problem
on a case-by-case basis. I had very little experience with systematic
right-hand drilling (aside from the alternating bass rudiments mentioned
above).

Over about seven months, I worked through the [120
exercises][guiliani_120_gp_file] in small increments, typically about five
minutes a day. The effect was real. The ring finger improvment was the most
noticeable. I select the ring finger as a first resort much more often now,
even to the point of adjusting my right-hand choices on tunes that I have
played for years. I can hear the difference in stability and tone. More
broadly, my right hand feels generally more settled and in control. If you
come from a non-classical tradition and have been building technique one song
at a time, these exercises might be worth your time.

## Practical matters

<span class="phead">Sources</span>. I created a Guitar Pro file using various
tablature files I found online. My file might have errors or discrepancies
relative to a more authoritative source. I did my work carefully, but did not
aim for perfection. For video performances of all of the exercises, see this
[playlist][scarfullery_playlist] from Rafael Scarfullery.

<span class="phead">Notation</span>. Right-hand fingering uses pima-style
notation: `p` for thumb, `i` for index, `m` for middle, and `a` for ring. In
the file I do not exaustively label every note but instead convey the
right-hand fingering as economically and intuitively as I can.

<span class="phead">Practicing advice</span>. Work on this kind of project in
consistent, small chunks. Do not fixate on speed or assume that you must reach
any particular speed. Instead, aim for control and clarity at whatever speed
is appropriate for your current skill level. Start by ensuring that you can
make the chord shifts fluently: the C chord will be the easiest for most
beginner and intermediate guitarists; the G7 voicing is likely to be less
familiar and more challenging. Finally, try to play the exercises as musically
as you can: muting, stacatto, dynamics, and fermata are all available; deploy
them where your ear tells you they will help.

<span class="phead">Other resources: Robertson</span>. I cannot recommend
highly enough Brooks Robertson's Truefire course on [fingerstyle
rolls][fingerstyle_rolls.]. It focuses on rolls — one of the primary
right-hand techniques covered in these Giuliani etudes -- but does so in a
more modern fingerstyle vein. The teaching is well done and the etudes he
crafts are both challenging and musically compelling. I had a lot of fun
getting them up to speed.

<span class="phead">Other resources: Davis</span>. Christopher Davis created a
PDF called [Guiliani 120+][guiliani_120_plus] that provides the Guiliani
etudes organized generally by difficulty. He also provides additional etudes
and some practicing tips. Davis provides the etudes with standard notation,
not tablature.

## Skills and examples

My previous rudiments post took an algorithmic approach — starting from a
chord progression and a few rules, it generated every possible pattern (256 of
them). Giuliani's 120 work differently. He rarely exhausts the combinatorial
space; instead he selects representative examples for most skills, reserving
the full-coverage treatment for the exercises he apparently considered most
critical.

### Rolls

Giuliani opens with the simplest right-hand skill: a sequential roll. For
example, etude #2 uses what Brooks Robertson would call a forward roll,
`p-i-m`, where the index and middle fingers play the top two strings and the
bass moves across different strings.

    <img_2>

Many other variations are covered. Etude #4 moves the roll itself across
different string sets.

    <img_4>

Etude #6 does the same, but uses what Robertson calls a mixed roll, `p-m-i`.

    <img_6>

And edude #10 combines various rolls, mixed (`p-m-i`) and backward (`a-m-i`).
This example is both doable but challenging, and an early example of how these
etudes require right-hand skills that the usual fingerstyle picking patterns
do not cover.

    <img_10>

The collection also includes grand rolls covering more than three strings. For
example, edude #90 uses five strings and includes both a mixed roll of
`p-a-m-i` and a forward roll of `p-i-m-i`.

    <img_90>

The larger rolls also use what pick guitarists would describe as economy or
sweep picking, repeating the thumb on the way up and the index finger on the
way down. Etude #89 is one example.

    <img_89>

### Pinches and compound patterns

Giuliani covers another building block familiar to fingerstyle players:
the pinch, where the thumb and fingers play at the same time.
Etude #11 is a simple example in this vein, combining down-beat pinches with
forward rolls.

    <img_11>

As the exercises advance, the pinch expands: instead of two simultaneous
strings, three or more sound at once, producing a partial chord. Etude
18 opens with a `pma` chord hit followed by a `p-i` pair.

    <img_18>

A more difficult example is edude #23, which uses more compound pinches and
places them both on and off the down beats. With exercises like this you have
to work on controlling fingers individually rather than falling into a routine
of plucking all fingers as a block

    <img_23>

### Finger pairs

Classical single-note technique runs heavily on strict alternation between
two fingers, especially `i-m` and `p-i`.

Etude #17 is a good illustration on the simpler side of the spectrum. It

### Thumb independence

The group of etudes #51-65 is one of the more demanding for
non-classical players. A steady `im` is played on every 16th note
and the bass moves across different strings. When the bass lands
on the down beats, things feel familiar, as in etude #51.

    <img_51>

But when the bass lands on multiple off-beats, as in etude #56,
things get quite tricky.

    <img_56>

### Chord placement

Etudes 66-80 alter the dynamic. In these, the lower voice is consistent across
all of the exercises: the bass plays string 5, 4, 3, 4 repeatedly. What varies
is the rhythmic placement of a pair of upper notes. The challenge is placing a
compound event accurately at any position in the measure without disrupting
the running bass line beneath it.
