# RH rudiments: Giuliani 120

## Inventory

Skills:
  - Rolls (arpeggio patterns): sequential single-note rolls: pim or pima.
    - Static or ascending/descending string sets.
      - 2 pim; static string set
      - 4 pim; ascending string sets
    - Ascending/descending rolls.
      - 5 mip; ascending string sets; descending roll  # challenge
    - Simple or mixed etudes:
      - 10 pmi ami pmi  # challenge
    - full pima
      - 90 p a m i p i m a

  - Pinch plus roll:
    - Like the etudes above, but the rolls use ima and the thumb also
      plays on one or more beats within the roll.
    - Rolls can vary (as above).
    - Pinch beats can vary.
    - Either simple or mixed etudes patterns.
    - Examples:
      - 11 ima, pinch beat 1
      - 16 pmi ami

  - Compound patterns: multiple fingers simultaneously (partial chords)
      - Similar to pinches, but more than 2 fingers in the pinch
      - As etudes advance, they tend to use multiple techniques
      - Examples:
        - 18 pma p i : 3-note pinch then p i
        - 23 pma p ima  # More complex

  - Grand roll:
    - Like rolls, but across more strings so one repeats p during
      ascent and i during descent.
    - 89 p p i m a m i i

  - Finger pairs:
    - With thumb:
      - 17 pi : basic example
      - 98 pi mi
      - 101 pi ai ai ai mi pi pi pi
    - Without thumb (classic is i-m alternation):
      - 81 p i m i
      - 82 p m i m
      - 83 p i m i  pa i m i
      - 85 p i a i  p i m i
    - Combo:
      - 25 pi mi pi  # fun and a good exercise to get thumb to higher strings
      - 27 pi pi mi pm im am
      - 29 pi ai mi
      - 30 pi pm ia   pa im pi   # various techniques
      - These combos verge into rolls.
        - 31 pi ma mi   # pim ami
        - 32 pa mi ma   # pam ima

  - Tremolo preparation: patterns like p-a-m-i
    - 100 p a m i

  - Alternating bass:
    - 36-50
    - Examples:
      - 36 pm i p i    # simple
      - 40 pm im p i   # harder

  - Thumb independence:
    - Static interval with im on every 16th note in 4/4 time.
    - The bass moves around -- asending, descending.
    - Bass also hits non-intutive beats -- ie not first 16th in each 4-note cluster.
    - 51-65
      - G really covers most of the combos.
      - He doesn't do that with other families of etudes.
    - Examples:
      - 51 pim im im im   # Simple
      - 54 im im im pim   # Bass on 4th note
      - 56 im pim pim im  # tricky combos: bass on 2nd and 3rd

  - Chord event independence (or chord insertion independence):
    - 66-80
    - Lower voice (via p and i) is the backbone across the batch.
    - 3-note chord hits are the variable, coming on various beat(s).
    - Examples:
      - 66 pma p i p
      - 68 p p ima p
      - 78 pma p ima pma

Favorites exercising various skills:
  - 91: several techniques; fun to get up to speed
  - 94: ditto: roll plus economy plus motion
  - 95: mi and pi workout; lost of ascend/descend
  - 96: pi then pami (tremelo)
  - 101: finger-pair workout

  - Title: Guiliani 120+
  - Link: https://www.classicalguitar.org/freemusic/exercises/Giuliani120.pdf
  - Provides all 120 etudes in standard notation.
  - Groups them into levels.
  - Provides additional etudes for some levels.

  - Levels:
    - Organized by difficulty and purpose.

      - I struggled to align well with Davis' groupings.

## Draft: Opening Section

The [first post][fingerstyle-rh-rudiments] at The Fifth Fret covered
right-hand rudiments for fingerstyle guitar: a set of 256 exercises I created
to build fluency in the alternating-bass patterns common to blues and folk
picking. This post is a continuation of that endeavor — focusing on right-hand
technique — but the source material is different: classical guitar etudes
written over two centuries ago.

[Mauro Giuliani][wiki-giuliani] was one of the leading classical guitarists of
the early 19th century. His "120 Right-Hand Studies" (Op. 1, Part 1) were
published in 1812 and have been a staple of classical guitar education ever
since. Segovia reportedly used them as a daily warmup. The design is simple:
every etude consists of two bars, one of C and one of G7. The early exercises
tend to focus on specific right-hand patterns or skills. The latter exercises
combine those patterns in various ways.

Collectively, the 120 etudes leave few corners of right-hand technique
unexplored. A steady diet of tunes, even a varied one, tends to favor your
existing strengths and route around your weaknesses. These exercises don't.
They amount to a right-hand audit by a teacher with centuries of expert
endorsement. [the phrasing is a bit awkward, but the idea seems useful: G is
not some random dude from the past; he's a teacher whose approach has been
validated by decade after decade of people working on the front lines of
guitar education].

I came to these exercises as an intermediate fingerstyle player -- blues,
folk, and some jazz, roughly in the vein of Tommy Emmanuel. I had years of
exerience, but was mainly self-taught via books and then internet. My right hand
policy, to the extent that I had one, was to favor the strongest fingers —
index and middle -- and to resort to the ring finger only when a tune demanded
it. In addition to some known weakness in the ring finger, I also lacked the
ability to play strictly alternating index-middle single-note lines on the
guitar — a workhorse of classical guitar technique. Instead, I would select
right-hand fingering intuitively, often relying on what one might call economy
(or rake/sweep) fingering in the right-hand, such as repeating the index
finger while descending from string to string. I could not 
consistenly play full scale patterns or long lines with strict index-middle
alternation. Finally, if a right-hand maneuver caused any trouble when
learning a song, I would solve each problem on a case-by-case basis. I had
very little experience with any kind of systematic right-hand drilling (aside
from the alternating bass rudiments mentioned above).

Over about seven months, I worked through the 120 exercises in small
increments, typically about five minutes a day. The effect was real. The ring
finger improvment was the most noticeable. I select the ring finger as a first
resort much more often now, even to the point of adjusting my right-hand
choices on tunes that I have played for years. I can hear the difference in
stability and tone. More broadly, my right hand feels more settled and in
control in a way I find hard to describe precisely. If you come from a
non-classical tradition and have been building technique one song at a time,
these exercises might be worth your time.

## Draft: Summary of skills/examples

My earlier rudiments post took an algorithmic approach — starting from
a progression and rules, then generating every possible pattern (256
of them). Giuliani's 120 work differently. He rarely exhausts the
combinatorial space; instead he selects representative examples for
most skills, reserving the full-coverage treatment for the exercises
he apparently considered most critical.

## Draft: conclusion

...

The typical online guitar curriculum — CAGED, scale patterns, chord
voicings, song tutorials — is not really in the business of systematic
technique building. It teaches you things to play. Classical guitar pedagogy,
by contrast, has spent two centuries figuring out how to build a player's
technical foundation from the ground up. The Giuliani 120 are a product of
that tradition.

You don't need to want to play classical guitar to benefit from them. I had no
such ambition. But working through exercises designed by someone with that
kind of pedagogical depth -- even five minutes a day -- turns out to be a
different kind of investment than learning another song. The Guitar Pro file
is linked above if you want to give them a try.
