## Drills of a different color

The [first post][fingerstyle-rh-rudiments] at The Fifth Fret covered
right-hand rudiments for fingerstyle guitar: a set of 256 exercises I created
to build fluency in the alternating-bass patterns common to blues and folk
picking. This post is a continuation of that endeavor — focusing on right-hand
technique — but the source material is different: classical guitar etudes
written over two centuries ago.

[Mauro Giuliani][wiki-giuliani] was one of the leading classical guitarists of
the early 19th century. His "120 Right-Hand Studies" (Op. 1, Part 1) were
published in 1812 and have been a staple of classical guitar education ever
since. Segovia reportedly used them as a daily warmup. The design is simple:
every etude consists of two bars, one of C and one of G7. The early exercises
tend to focus on specific right-hand patterns or skills. The latter exercises
combine those patterns in various ways.

Collectively, the 120 etudes leave few corners of right-hand technique
unexplored. A steady diet of tunes, even a varied one, tends to favor your
existing strengths and route around your weaknesses. These exercises don't.
They amount to a right-hand audit by a teacher with centuries of expert
endorsement. [the phrasing is a bit awkward, but the idea seems useful: G is
not some random dude from the past; he's a teacher whose approach has been
validated by decade after decade of people working on the front lines of
guitar education].

I came to these exercises as an intermediate fingerstyle player -- blues,
folk, and some jazz, roughly in the vein of Tommy Emmanuel. I had years of
exerience, but was mainly self-taught via books and then internet. My right hand
policy, to the extent that I had one, was to favor the strongest fingers —
index and middle -- and to resort to the ring finger only when a tune demanded
it. In addition to some known weakness in the ring finger, I also lacked the
ability to play strictly alternating index-middle single-note lines on the
guitar — a workhorse of classical guitar technique. Instead, I would select
right-hand fingering intuitively, often relying on what one might call economy
(or rake/sweep) fingering in the right-hand, such as repeating the index
finger while descending from string to string. I could not
consistenly play full scale patterns or long lines with strict index-middle
alternation. Finally, if a right-hand maneuver caused any trouble when
learning a song, I would solve each problem on a case-by-case basis. I had
very little experience with any kind of systematic right-hand drilling (aside
from the alternating bass rudiments mentioned above).

Over about seven months, I worked through the 120 exercises in small
increments, typically about five minutes a day. The effect was real. The ring
finger improvment was the most noticeable. I select the ring finger as a first
resort much more often now, even to the point of adjusting my right-hand
choices on tunes that I have played for years. I can hear the difference in
stability and tone. More broadly, my right hand feels more settled and in
control in a way I find hard to describe precisely. If you come from a
non-classical tradition and have been building technique one song at a time,
these exercises might be worth your time.

## Skills and examples

My earlier rudiments post took an algorithmic approach — starting from a
progression and rules, then generating every possible pattern (256 of them).
Giuliani's 120 work differently. He rarely exhausts the combinatorial space;
instead he selects representative examples for most skills, reserving the
full-coverage treatment for the exercises he apparently considered most
critical.

Some terminology:

  - string sets
  - pima: finger names
  - string set motion: a movement repeated across different sets of strings
  - roll
  - pinch

### Rolls

Giuliani opens with the simplest right-hand gesture: a sequential roll
through some subset of `p`, `i`, `m` across a string set. The early
exercises work through every meaningful permutation — `p i m`, `p m i`,
`m i p`, and others — over static and then shifting string positions.

        - 2 pim; static i-m strings, with moving bass
        - 4 pim; ascending string sets for the entire roll
        - 6 pmi: like 4, but a mixed roll:
          - super important technique
          - also the ending-i and starting-p are on same string
          - rolls do not have to occur on different strings.
        - 10: combo of various rolls:
          - fun challenge: pmi ami pmi
          - early and doable example of how these etudes require movements
            that the usual pattern picking examples lack
        - 90: full pima: p a m i p i m a

### Pinch + roll

A pinch — the simultaneous pluck of thumb and one finger — is familiar
to any fingerstyle player. Giuliani uses it as a building block, pairing
an opening pinch with a trailing roll. The pinch falls on beat one in
the simpler exercises; later entries vary which beat receives the pinch,
and some bars contain multiple pinches.

      - 11 ima, pinch beat 1

### Compound patterns

As the exercises advance, the pinch expands: instead of two simultaneous
strings, three or more sound at once, producing a partial chord. Etude
18 opens with a `pma` chord hit followed by a `p i` pair. Later examples
combine compound hits with rolls in the same bar.

        - 18 pma p i : 3-note pinch then p i
        - 23 pma p ima  # More complex

### Alternating bass

Any Travis-style player will recognize this territory: the thumb
alternates between two bass strings while the fingers fill in between.
Etudes 36-50 drill this systematically, moving from simple versions to
entries that layer additional finger activity on top of the alternating
thumb.

G's alt-bass approach has its familiar part for fingerpickers (the
alt bass), but also different: melody finger combos and rhythm
not typical of fingerstyle or pattern picking

      - 36 pm i p i    # simple; familiar in fingerstyle
      - 37 p im p i    # not difficult; but uncommon for fingerstyle
      - 40 pm im p i   # harder

### Grand roll / economy motion

When a roll spans five or six strings, repeating the thumb on the way up
and a finger on the way down becomes the efficient choice — what plectrum
players call economy or sweep picking. Giuliani includes exercises that
make this explicit, treating the repeated digit as a technique to drill
rather than a habit to avoid.

    - 89 p p i m a m i i

### Finger pairs

Classical single-note technique runs heavily on strict alternation between
two fingers, with no repeats. Giuliani introduces finger-pair exercises
early and returns to them throughout. The simplest examples are `p i` or
`p m` pairs; later entries work through less common combinations including
the ring finger, and some bars mix several different pairs.

      - With thumb: pi and pm
        - 17 : basic example to work on pi or pm
        - 98 pi mi: pairs on same string; plus ascending motion

      - Without thumb (classic is i-m alternation):
        - 81 p imi: classic i-m
        - 82 p mim: same, but m-i

      - Combo: all fun
        - 25 pi mi pi  # good exercise to get thumb to higher strings
        - 27 pi pi mi pm im am: pairs plus rolls

### Thumb independence

Etudes 51-65 is one of the more demanding sections for a non-classical player.
A steady `im` interval occursd on every 16th note throughout each bar.
The thumb must insert bass notes at varying positions — including on the
second or third 16th of a four-note cluster, positions with no natural
metric anchor. Getting the thumb to fire off-beat while the fingers run
on autopilot is a genuine coordination challenge.

Giuliani is unusually thorough here, covering nearly every possible bass
placement across the batch. Most other sections in the 120 use
representative samples rather than exhaustive coverage; the systematic
treatment signals how seriously he weighted this skill.

        - 51 pim im im im   # Simple
        - 56 im pim pim im  # tricky combos: bass on 2nd and 3rd

### Chord placement

Etudes 66-80 reverse the dynamic of the previous section. A lower-voice
pattern — thumb and index cycling through a fixed string sequence — runs as
the backbone, and a three-note chord hit must land at varying positions across
the bar. The challenge is placing a compound event accurately at any metric
position without disrupting the running bass line beneath it.

        - 66 pma p i p
        - 69 p p p pma  # more challenging variant
        - 78 pma p ima pma # multiple chord-events per 4-note group

### Favorites

    - 91: several techniques; fun to get up to speed
    - 94: ditto: roll + economy + motion
    - 95: mi and pi pairs + motion
    - 96: pi then pami (beginner step for tremelo)
    - 101: finger-pair workout + melodic motion in upper and lower registers

## Practical advice

Also:
  - Sources
  - video examples
    - https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL3qRaKhDtIvRRgoEVZVqaFu6xFjBUNrIo
  - My RH notation
  - make it musical: for example:
    - last chord:
      - roll: ppima or ppppp
      - fermata before
    - stacatto or muting where is sounds better
  - excellent course in fingerpicking genre focused on rolls: Brooks Robertson
  - make sure you can perform the chord shift well first
    - the C shape will be easiest for most beginner/intermediate
    - the G7 shape is trickier: not a common fingerstyle grip; involves pinky

Take it in consistent, small chunks. Don't aim for ultimate speed.
Do aim for control at whatever level your ability can support.

I found G's ordering to be logical and sensible, but classical
teachers with more experience might have good suggestions
for a different ordering. One example I found:

    - Title: Guiliani 120+
    - Link: https://www.classicalguitar.org/freemusic/exercises/Giuliani120.pdf

Davis provides all 120 etudes in standard notation and groups them into
levels. For some levels he provides additional etudes. His groupings seem to
be driven mostly by difficulty and also by skill or purpose. That said, I
could not make complete sense of his rationale for the groups in all cases.
G's grouping was more obvious to me than Davis, but I'm coming at this subject
with limited exposure to classical guitar technique. As best I can tell, G's
organization priorities technique/skill more than difficulty. Davis flips the
two criteria.

He also provides practice tips. Two in particular align with my experience:
(a) slow then fast (speed bursts rather than raw speed overall); (b) isolate
RH only (open strings or fully muted). I get the most out of fully muted.

## Classical technique: an antidote to modern guitar teaching

The typical online guitar curriculum — CAGED, scale patterns, chord
voicings, song tutorials — is not really in the business of systematic
technique building. It teaches you things to play. Classical guitar pedagogy,
by contrast, has spent two centuries figuring out how to build a player's
technical foundation from the ground up. The Giuliani 120 are a product of
that tradition.

You don't need to want to play classical guitar to benefit from them. I had no
such ambition. But working through exercises designed by someone with that
kind of pedagogical depth -- even five minutes a day -- turns out to be a
different kind of investment than learning another song. The Guitar Pro file
is linked above if you want to give them a try.
