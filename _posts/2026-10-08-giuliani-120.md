## Drills of a different color

The [first post][fingerstyle_rh_rudiments] at The Fifth Fret covered
right-hand rudiments for fingerstyle guitar: a set of 256 exercises I created
to build fluency in the alternating-bass patterns common to blues and folk
picking. This post is a continuation of that endeavor, but the source material
is different: classical guitar etudes written over two centuries ago.

[Mauro Giuliani][wiki_giuliani] was one of the leading classical guitarists of
the early 19th century. His "120 Right-Hand Studies" (Op. 1, Part 1) were
published in 1812 and have been a staple of classical guitar education ever
since. The design is simple: every etude consists of two bars, one of C and
one of G7. The early exercises tend to focus on specific right-hand patterns
or skills. The later exercises combine those patterns in various ways.

Outside a few technique-minded niches, such as bluegrass and shred guitar, the
online guitar world is a sea of things to play, with only a few islands of
rigorous technique building. And a steady diet of tunes, even a varied one,
tends to favor your existing strengths and route around your weaknesses. These
exercises do not let you route around anything; few corners of right-hand
technique go unexplored.

I came to the etudes as an intermediate fingerstyle player, roughly in the
vein of Tommy Emmanuel (blues, folk, some jazz). I had years of experience,
but was mainly self-taught via books and then the internet. My right-hand
policy, to the extent that I had one, was to favor the strongest fingers,
index and middle, and to resort to the ring finger only when a tune demanded
it. I could not play single-note lines with strict index-middle alternation —
a workhorse of classical guitar technique. Instead, I would select right-hand
fingering intuitively, often repeating the same finger on one string and
bumping up against the speed limit that approach entails. I also overrelied on
economy (or rake/sweep) picking, such as repeating the index finger while
descending from string to string. Finally, when a right-hand maneuver gave me
trouble while learning a song, I solved it on a case-by-case basis. I had
little experience with systematic right-hand drilling (aside from the
alternating-bass rudiments mentioned above).

Over about seven months, I worked through the 120 exercises in small
increments, typically five minutes a day. The effect was real. The biggest
improvement was in my ring finger. I select it as a first resort much more
often now, even to the point of adjusting my right-hand choices on tunes that
I have played for years. I can hear the difference in stability and tone. More
broadly, my right hand feels more settled and in control. My index-middle
alternation improved too, enough to give me an alternative when same-finger
repetition is too slow, but it took a different set of Giuliani etudes to
really lock it in — a topic for a future post. If you come from a
non-classical tradition and have been building technique one song at a time,
these exercises might be worth your time.

## Practical matters

<span class="phead">Sources</span>. I created the [Guitar Pro
file][giuliani_120_gp_file] using various tablature files I found online. My
file might have errors or discrepancies relative to a more [authoritative
sources][imslp_sources]. I did my work carefully, but did not aim for
perfection. For video performances of all of the exercises, see this
[playlist][scarfullery_playlist] from Rafael Scarfullery.

<span class="phead">Notation</span>. Right-hand fingering uses pima-style
notation: `p` for thumb, `i` for index, `m` for middle, and `a` for ring. The
file labels fingering as economically as I could manage, rather than on every
note.

<span class="phead">Practice advice</span>. Work on this kind of project in
consistent, small chunks. I practiced the etudes in order, using the Guitar
Pro playback to internalize the sound of each before playing it. Start by
ensuring that you can make the chord shifts fluently. The C chord will be the
easiest for most beginner and intermediate guitarists; the G7 voicing is
likely to be a bit more difficult. Do not fixate on speed or assume that you
must reach any particular tempo. Instead, aim for control and clarity at
whatever speed you can manage. Finally, try to play the exercises as musically
as you can: muting, staccato, dynamics, and fermatas are all available; deploy
them where your ear tells you they will help.

<span class="phead">Other resources: Robertson</span>. I highly recommend
Brooks Robertson's TrueFire course on [fingerstyle rolls][fingerstyle_rolls].
It focuses on one of the primary right-hand techniques covered in these
Giuliani etudes, but does so in a more modern fingerstyle vein. The teaching
is well done and the etudes he crafts are both challenging and musically
compelling. I had a lot of fun learning them.

<span class="phead">Other resources: Davis</span>. Christopher Davis created a
PDF called [Giuliani 120+][giuliani_120_plus] that organizes the Giuliani
etudes by difficulty. He also provides additional etudes and some practice
tips. The material uses standard notation, not tablature.

## Skills and examples

This section surveys some of the right-hand skills the etudes develop, with a
few examples of each. If you are on the fence about the value of practicing
the etudes, test yourself by trying several of the simpler ones. When I
started them, I was often unpleasantly surprised to find my fingers tripping
over each other on material I thought I could play.

### Rolls

Giuliani opens with the simplest right-hand skill: a sequential roll. For
example, etude 2 uses what Robertson would call a forward roll, `p-i-m`, where
the index and middle fingers play the top two strings and the bass moves
across different strings.

### Pinches

Giuliani covers another building block familiar to fingerstyle players: the
pinch, where the thumb and fingers play at the same time. Etude 11 is a simple
example, combining downbeat pinches with forward rolls.

{%
    include figure
    image_path="/assets/images/g120/ex-011.jpg"
    alt="Etude 11"
    caption="Etude 11"
%}

As the exercises advance, the pinch expands: instead of two simultaneous
strings, three or more sound at once, producing a partial chord. Etude 18
opens with a `pma` chord hit followed by a `p-i` pair.

A more difficult example is etude 23, which uses denser pinches and places
them both on and off the downbeats. Exercises like this force you to control

### Thumb independence

### Favorites

I kept a running list of favorites as I worked: etudes that sounded like music
rather than drills and that were fun to play, with fingers going brrrr along
an interesting path. Only while writing this post did I notice that they all
lean on finger pairs. Maybe that is no accident. Alternating pairs give the
line freedom to move: rocking back and forth across adjacent strings,
traveling through the chord, or jumping two or three strings at a time.
