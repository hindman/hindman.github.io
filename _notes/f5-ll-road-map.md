## Guilian 120 RH exercises

### Basics

Duration:
  - First pass:
    - Started: early July.
    - Finished: end of Jan.

### Inventory

Skills:
  - Rolls (arpeggio patterns): sequential single-note rolls: pim or pima.
    - Static or ascending/descending string sets.
      - 2 pim; static string set
      - 4 pim; ascending string sets
    - Ascending/descending rolls.
      - 5 mip; ascending string sets; descending roll  # challenge
    - Simple or mixed etudes:
      - 10 pmi ami pmi  # challenge
    - full pima
      - 90 p a m i p i m a

  - Pinch plus roll:
    - Like the etudes above, but the rolls use ima and the thumb also
      plays on one or more beats within the roll.
    - Rolls can vary (as above).
    - Pinch beats can vary.
    - Either simple or mixed etudes patterns.
    - Examples:
      - 11 ima, pinch beat 1
      - 16 pmi ami

  - Compound patterns: multiple fingers simultaneously (partial chords)
      - Similar to pinches, but more than 2 fingers in the pinch
      - As etudes advance, they tend to use multiple techniques
      - Examples:
        - 18 pma p i : 3-note pinch then p i
        - 23 pma p ima  # More complex

  - Grand roll:
    - Like rolls, but across more strings so one repeats p during
      ascent and i during descent.
    - 89 p p i m a m i i

  - Finger pairs:
    - With thumb:
      - 17 pi : basic example
      - 98 pi mi
      - 101 pi ai ai ai mi pi pi pi
    - Without thumb (classic is i-m alternation):
      - 81 p i m i
      - 82 p m i m
      - 83 p i m i  pa i m i
      - 85 p i a i  p i m i
    - Combo:
      - 25 pi mi pi  # fun and a good exercise to get thumb to higher strings
      - 27 pi pi mi pm im am
      - 29 pi ai mi
      - 30 pi pm ia   pa im pi   # various techniques
      - These combos verge into rolls.
        - 31 pi ma mi   # pim ami
        - 32 pa mi ma   # pam ima

  - Tremolo preparation: patterns like p-a-m-i
    - 100 p a m i

  - Alternating bass:
    - 36-50
    - Examples:
      - 36 pm i p i    # simple
      - 40 pm im p i   # harder

  - Thumb independence:
    - Static interval with im on every 16th note in 4/4 time.
    - The bass moves around -- asending, descending.
    - Bass also hits non-intutive beats -- ie not first 16th in each 4-note cluster.
    - 51-65
      - G really covers most of the combos.
      - He doesn't do that with other families of etudes.
    - Examples:
      - 51 pim im im im   # Simple
      - 54 im im im pim   # Bass on 4th note
      - 56 im pim pim im  # tricky combos: bass on 2nd and 3rd

  - Chord event independence (or chord insertion independence):
    - 66-80
    - Lower voice (via p and i) is the backbone across the batch.
    - 3-note chord hits are the variable, coming on various beat(s).
    - Examples:
      - 66 pma p i p
      - 68 p p ima p
      - 78 pma p ima pma

Favorites exercising various skills:
  - 91: several techniques; fun to get up to speed
  - 94: ditto: roll plus economy plus motion
  - 95: mi and pi workout; lost of ascend/descend
  - 96: pi then pami (tremelo)
  - 101: finger-pair workout

I'm starting to work on a new post for The Fifth Fret (F5). The topic
is Giuliani's 120 studies for right hand:

  - I have a .gp file of all of them to share with others.

  - I worked through the exercises and got a lot out of them and want to try
    to explain my sense what it did for someone in my situation.

  - My background. Quite a bit of fingerstyle experience as a hobbyist; but
    self/internet/book taught so no extensive technical work.
  - In particular, my RH approach was to favor strong fingers: if i-m can do
    it, use them; otherwise, involve finger-a only when needed.
  - Plus some basic facility in p-i picking.
  - Known weaker areas before starting:
    - Finger-a not as confident, consistent, fluid. Not hopeless, but a clear
      weak spot.
    - Lacked ability to play i-m and m-i consistently. Tendency to rely on
      repeating same finger across descending string shifts (drag, rake,
      sweep, or economy fingering). My RH finger choices were mostly intuitive
      -- aside from the most troublesome passages, where I would/could work
      out a needed fingering. But this was not a setting I could control with
      full accuracy: eg, play a 2-octave scale up and down with strict i-m or
      m-i.
    - Some specific finger combos were tough. These would crop up when
      learning new tunes, and I would solve each specific case at hand.

  - The 120 exercises did not revolutionize things for me.
    - But I did notice real changes in a positive direction.
    - Finger-a much improved.
      - I find myself adjusting how I play songs I've known for years, and I
        can percieve and hear the improvement: more stability and confidence
        and/or better sound over some of the passages where I stopped working
        around finger-a.
      - On new tunes I'm much more likely to use finger-a than I would have in
        the past.
    - Some i-m/m-i improvement.
      - I perceived this effect as real but less striking than the one for
        finger-a.
      - I'll return to this subject in another post.

    - More significant but also a bit mysterious:
      - My RH is just better.
      - I perceive it as more settled, more in control.

=====================

How teachers tend to group them. The categories aren't always agreed upon, but
the most common breakdown by skill:

  - Thumb-finger pairs: p+i, p+m, p+a in isolation — early studies
  - Arpeggio patterns: sequential single-note rolls across strings, various
    orderings of i-m-a with bass thumb
  - Alternation: strict i-m and m-i on single or adjacent strings
  - Ring finger development: patterns where a carries melodic weight rather
    than inner voice
  - Compound patterns: multiple fingers plucking simultaneously (partial
    chords), often p + two fingers
  - Tremolo preparation: patterns like p-a-m-i that build toward tremolo
    technique
